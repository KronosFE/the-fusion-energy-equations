Nomenclature & Glossary

This book asks you to follow a real argument built out of real numbers, and real numbers come with real vocabulary. Some of the words here are borrowed from plasma physics, some from power engineering, and a handful are specific to how this program is built — two machines, one that makes fuel and one that burns it. I have tried to keep every entry to a sentence or two, in plain language, and to say what a term actually means rather than what it is often marketed to mean. Where a word carries a common misunderstanding, I flag it.

A note on how to read the table. The entries are alphabetical so you can jump in and out. You do not have to read it end to end; treat it as a place to return to whenever a chapter throws a term at you faster than it defines it. The units matter more than they look, especially the difference between fusion power, thermal power, and electricity — that single distinction is where a lot of fusion optimism quietly hides, and where this book tries hard not to.

TermMeaning
AegisThe name for the burner in its fixed-defense-site housing: firm, dispatchable power for installations that cannot tolerate an intermittent supply. It is not a naval system. The reactor inside is the same D–³He tandem mirror as MetroVolt.
Alpha particle (α)A helium nucleus (two protons, two neutrons) born charged and fast in many fusion reactions. Because it is charged, its energy can be captured directly or used to heat the plasma, rather than escaping as a neutron.
AneutronicA fusion reaction that releases most of its energy as charged particles rather than fast neutrons. No reaction is perfectly aneutronic; in practice it means low-neutron, which is the honest way to describe the burner fuel here.
Aspect ratioThe ratio of a toroidal machine's major radius (the size of the ring) to its minor radius (the fatness of the tube). A low aspect ratio makes the "cored-apple" shape of a spherical tokamak and can improve how efficiently the field confines the plasma.
AUC"Area under the curve," a single score from 0.5 (random guessing) to 1.0 (perfect) for how well a classifier separates two outcomes. We use it to grade disruption prediction; the disruption ensemble scores 0.990.
Beta (β)The ratio of the plasma's pressure to the pressure of the magnetic field holding it. It measures how much plasma you are confining per unit of magnetic effort; higher beta is more economical but harder to keep stable.
BlanketThe layer surrounding the plasma that absorbs fusion neutrons, turns their energy into heat, and — in a breeder — carries lithium so that neutrons transmute it into fresh tritium. The blanket is where the breeder's fuel-making actually happens.
BOAK"Best-of-a-kind." An informal marker for the most refined unit a program can currently build — the target that first and later units are measured against.
Break-evenThe point where the fusion power produced equals the heating power put into the plasma — scientific gain Q = 1. It is a physics milestone, not a commercial one: a plant must run far past break-even to send net electricity to the grid.
Bootstrap currentA current a confined plasma drives through itself, free of charge, out of its own pressure gradients — discovered during the long night and now a load-bearing gift in every modern tokamak design, this book's breeder included.
BremsstrahlungGerman for "braking radiation": the X-rays a plasma emits when electrons are deflected by ions. It is an unavoidable energy loss that rises steeply with temperature and with heavier ions, and it sets a hard ceiling on which fuels can ever burn.
Central cellThe long central section of a tandem mirror where most of the burning happens, held between the two high-field end plugs. In the burner it runs at about 17 T and is roughly the length of the machine.
Charged particleAny product of a reaction that carries electric charge — an alpha, a proton, a helium-3 nucleus. Charged particles can be steered and slowed by fields, which is why a low-neutron fuel can feed direct energy conversion; neutrons, being neutral, cannot.
Control-barrier functionA control-theory tool that defines a "safe set" and mathematically guarantees the controller never steers the system across its boundary. In our AI stack it acts as a safety clamp on the plasma; in testing it caught 3,000 of 3,000 injected limit violations with zero escapes over 150,000 steps.
Cryostat / cryogenicsThe insulated vessel and cooling system that keep the superconducting magnets cold enough to carry current without loss. High-temperature superconductors relax how cold that must be, but the machine is still, at its heart, a very large, very precise refrigerator.
Deuterium (D)A heavy, stable isotope of hydrogen with one proton and one neutron. It is abundant in ordinary seawater and is one half of the breeder's D–T fuel and of the burner's D–³He fuel.
Deuterium–helium-3 (D–³He)The burner's fuel: deuterium fused with helium-3. Its great virtue is that the main reaction channel is low-neutron, releasing most of its energy as charged particles — which is what allows direct energy conversion and lighter shielding. Its cost is that it needs scarce helium-3 and extreme fields.
Deuterium–tritium (D–T)The breeder's fuel: deuterium fused with tritium. It is the easiest fusion reaction to ignite, which is why the breeder uses it — but it throws off most of its energy as fast neutrons, which is exactly what a breeder wants and a power plant does not.
Digital twinA continuously updated software model of a physical machine, fast and accurate enough to run alongside the real thing for prediction, testing, and control. Ours lean on surrogate models so they can run in real time.
Direct energy conversion (DEC)Capturing the energy of charged fusion products as electricity directly — decelerating them against an electric field — instead of first turning their energy into heat and running a steam cycle. It is what makes a low-neutron burner attractive, because the energy arrives as charged particles that can be collected.
DisruptionA sudden, uncontrolled collapse of a confined plasma that dumps its energy into the machine. Predicting and avoiding disruptions is one of the central jobs of the control system.
DivertorThe part of the machine that receives the plasma's exhaust — heat and spent fuel — at its edge, protecting the main wall. It faces some of the most severe heat loads in the whole device.
DOI / depositA "digital object identifier" is a permanent address for a published dataset or analysis. When this book says a result is deposited, it means the underlying numbers live at a fixed DOI where anyone can pull them and rerun the work, rather than being taken on faith.
Driven (vs. ignition)A burn that must be continuously fed external heating power to keep going. It is a legitimate and often preferable operating mode; the burner here is designed to run driven rather than chase ignition.
ELM"Edge-localized mode," a repetitive burst of energy and particles thrown off the edge of a high-confinement plasma. Large ELMs can damage plasma-facing components, so suppressing or avoiding them is a design priority.
End plugThe high-field magnetic "cork" at each end of a tandem mirror that keeps plasma from streaming out the open ends. Holding enough density at the plugs is the burner's one remaining hard physics gate; the burner's plugs reach a peak field near 26.49 T.
Energy confinement time (τ_E)How long the plasma holds onto its heat before it leaks away — roughly, the plasma's insulation quality. It is one of the three factors in the triple product, and improving it is one of the surest ways to raise a machine's gain.
Engineering gain (Q_eng)Gain measured at the wall plug rather than at the plasma: the electricity a plant sends out divided by all the electricity it takes to run. It is always a harsher number than scientific Q, and it is the one that decides whether a machine is a net power source. The breeder's is negative on purpose.
Firm powerElectricity that is available on demand, around the clock, regardless of weather or time of day. It is what a defense site or a data center actually needs, and it is the specific product the burner is built to sell — the opposite of intermittent supply.
FOAK / NOAK"First-of-a-kind" and "Nth-of-a-kind." FOAK units are expensive and slow because everything is being done for the first time; NOAK units are what a technology costs once it is built repeatedly and the learning curve has done its work. Honest energy forecasts must say which one they mean.
FoundryThe way this book describes the breeder: a plant whose product is materials — tritium, helium-3, fast neutrons — rather than electricity. Calling it a foundry rather than a power plant is the honest framing, because on power it runs at a net loss.
FusionThe joining of two light atomic nuclei into a heavier one, releasing energy because the product weighs slightly less than the ingredients. It is the reaction that powers the Sun, and forcing it to happen on Earth is what every machine in this book is for.
Greenwald limitAn empirical ceiling on how dense a tokamak plasma can be run for a given current before it tends to disrupt. It is one of the operational boundaries a control system must respect, and one of the "limit violations" a safety layer is tested against.
GyrokineticA class of physics models that resolve the fine-grained turbulence of a plasma by averaging over the fast circular motion of particles around field lines. They are extremely accurate and extremely expensive to run — a prime target for surrogate acceleration.
H-modeThe high-confinement regime a tokamak plasma can jump into, discovered in 1982: the edge spontaneously builds a transport barrier and confinement roughly doubles. One of the plasma's great acts of self-organization.
Helium-3 (³He / He-3)A rare, light isotope of helium with two protons and one neutron. It is the burner's low-neutron partner fuel; because it is scarce on Earth, the long-term plan sources it from the Moon (see He-3, lunar).
He-3, lunarHelium-3 deposited in the lunar soil by the solar wind over billions of years. It is the intended long-term supply for the burner fuel and the reason the roadmap reaches to the Moon around 2040; near-term He-3 also comes from the breeder itself.
High-temperature superconductor (HTS)A superconductor that carries loss-free current at temperatures far above the old liquid-helium regime, allowing much stronger, more compact magnets. See REBCO.
HyperionThe name of the breeder — a deuterium–tritium spherical tokamak whose job is to make fuel and materials, not sell electricity. It is the near-term product and the cash engine of the whole plan.
Ignition (vs. driven)A self-sustaining burn where the energy from fusion alone keeps the plasma hot, with no external heating needed. It is a celebrated goal, but not a requirement — a well-designed driven system can be simpler and more controllable.
LCOE"Levelized cost of electricity," the average price per unit of energy a plant must earn over its life to pay for itself. This book defines the term but deliberately keeps LCOE and all other cost figures out of the public book; the economics live elsewhere.
Lawson criterionThe classic condition for a net-energy fusion burn: the plasma density, temperature, and energy confinement time must together clear a threshold. It is the origin of the triple product (see below) and the yardstick every concept is measured against.
Magnetic confinementUsing strong magnetic fields to hold a hot plasma away from material walls, since no solid could survive contact. Both machines in this book are magnetic-confinement devices, of two different shapes.
Mass spectrographThe instrument (Aston, ~1920) precise enough to weigh individual atoms — the measurement that revealed four hydrogens outweigh one helium, and thereby where the stars get their energy.
MetroVoltThe name for the burner in its data-center housing: firm, clean, around-the-clock power for AI facilities. The reactor is identical to Aegis; what differs is the customer, and that this is where the long-run fleet — gated on lunar helium-3 — scales.
Mirror ratioIn a magnetic mirror, the ratio of the high field at the end plug to the lower field in the central cell. A higher mirror ratio corks the ends more effectively, trapping more of the plasma; it is part of why the burner needs such extreme plug fields.
MW / MWe / MW_fusMegawatts of power, but of three different kinds, and the difference is everything. MW_fus is the raw fusion power released; MW (thermal) is heat; MWe is electricity delivered. A machine can post a large MW_fus and still be net-negative in MWe — the breeder is, at about −79 MWe, and this book says so plainly.
Negative triangularityA plasma cross-section shaped like a backward "D," which can suppress edge instabilities such as ELMs and ease the heat-exhaust problem. The breeder runs at a triangularity of δ = −0.30.
Neutron activationThe process by which fusion neutrons make surrounding materials radioactive and brittle over time. It is the main reason neutrons are a liability for a power plant, and the reason a low-neutron fuel is worth its difficulty.
Neutron fraction (f_n)The share of a reaction's energy carried off by neutrons. High neutron fractions damage materials and demand heavy shielding; the burner's low fraction of about 5.44% is a central design advantage.
Open accessPublished so that anyone can read it without a subscription or a paywall. The Kronos design basis is deliberately open-access, because a claim the field cannot read is a claim the field cannot check.
PINN"Physics-informed neural network," a machine-learning model trained to obey the governing physical equations, not just fit data. We report an honest miss here: our PINN equilibrium solver reached 2.5% error against a 1% target.
PlasmaThe fourth state of matter — a gas hot enough that electrons are stripped from nuclei, leaving a charged, electrically conductive soup. Fusion happens in plasma, which is why so much of the engineering is really about holding one still.
Plasma-facing componentsThe surfaces that directly meet the plasma or its exhaust — first wall, divertor, and the like. They endure the harshest heat and particle loads in the machine, and their survival is a limiting factor on how hard a device can be pushed.
Q / gainThe ratio of fusion power out to heating power in. Q = 1 is break-even; Q greater than 1 means the plasma returns more than it is fed. The breeder's design scientific gain is about Q_sci ≈ 3.076.
Q40This program's physics target: a fortyfold energy return, Q = 40. It is the first of "the three forties" — Q40, $40 per MWh, within 40 years — and it belongs to the burner, not the breeder.
QuenchThe sudden, potentially damaging loss of superconductivity in a magnet, which turns a loss-free coil into a resistive one carrying enormous current. Detecting a quench early is critical; our fused precursor gives a 47-millisecond warning versus 2.6 milliseconds before.
REBCO"Rare-earth barium copper oxide," the high-temperature superconductor used for the magnets here. It enables very high fields in a compact machine — very high peak fields at the conductor in the breeder and a 26.49 T peak plug field in the burner.
ReproducibilityThe property that someone else, starting from the published record, can rerun the work and get the same answer. It is the standard this book holds itself to: every quantitative claim is meant to be reproducible from the open papers and deposits, not merely asserted.
Solar windThe steady stream of charged particles the Sun blows out into space. Over billions of years it has implanted helium-3 into the lunar soil, which is why the Moon — with no atmosphere to deflect it — is the intended long-term source of the burner's fuel.
Spherical tokamakA tokamak squashed toward the shape of a cored apple rather than a doughnut, with a tighter aspect ratio that can improve confinement efficiency. The breeder is a spherical tokamak.
Steam cycle (thermal conversion)The conventional way to make electricity: use heat to boil water, spin a turbine, drive a generator. It is what direct energy conversion aims to skip; every step of heat-to-motion-to-electricity loses energy, so collecting charged particles directly can be far more efficient.
StellaratorThe twisted, cruller-shaped magnetic bottle invented by Lyman Spitzer in 1951 — the tokamak's great sibling architecture, holding plasma with shaped external coils alone. Its three-dimensional design tradition trained the eyes that review this book's geometry.
Surrogate modelA fast approximation of a slow, expensive simulation, trained to reproduce its outputs in a fraction of the time. Ours run up to three orders of magnitude more accurately than prior methods for a given cost — the magnet twin by a factor of 7,475 — which is what makes real-time control possible.
Tandem mirrorA linear (straight, not ring-shaped) magnetic-confinement device that plugs both ends with high-field magnets to keep plasma from leaking out. The burner is a tandem mirror, roughly 440 meters end to end, with a direct-conversion train at its exit.
TBR (tritium breeding ratio)How many tritium atoms a machine's blanket breeds for each one its fusion reactions consume. It must exceed 1.0 for a D–T system to be self-sufficient in fuel; the breeder holds a deliberately marginal lever of 1.03–1.20.
Tesla (T)The unit of magnetic field strength. For scale, a strong hospital MRI is a few tesla; the breeder runs 8 T on-axis (substantially higher at the conductor) and the burner's plugs approach 26.49 T, which is why the magnet engineering is the hardest part of both machines.
ThermonuclearDescribing fusion driven by heat — by making a plasma hot enough that its nuclei collide fast enough to fuse. Every machine in this book is thermonuclear in this technical sense; the word simply means "fusion powered by temperature."
TokamakThe most mature magnetic-confinement design: a doughnut-shaped chamber that confines plasma with a combination of external magnets and a current driven through the plasma itself.
Triple productThe product of plasma density, temperature, and energy confinement time — the compact number that expresses the Lawson criterion. Raising it is the whole game of fusion; a concept lives or dies by how high a triple product it can reach and hold.
Tritium (T)A radioactive, short-lived isotope of hydrogen with one proton and two neutrons. It is the other half of D–T fuel and is scarce in nature, which is why a breeder must make its own — the breeder here produces roughly 4 kilograms of tritium per year.
