A Brighter Tomorrow

Come forward with me — not to a date I will promise you, because the honesty of this book is worth more than the drama of a year, but to the world that waits on the other side of a single number. Stand in it for a while. Walk its streets, drink its water, breathe its air. Everything you are about to see is downstream of two small, stubborn things, and I will hold them in front of you the whole way so the vision never floats free of them.

The first is a price: forty dollars a megawatt-hour — the line below which firm, carbon-free electricity stops being a premium good and becomes something closer to air, cheap enough that you stop rationing it and stop building your life around its scarcity. The second is a property, and it is the one the whole book was built to earn: firmness — power that runs day and night, in still air and cloud, through polar winter and monsoon, without ever once asking permission of the weather. Cheap intermittent power changes the electricity bill. Cheap firm power changes what you can build, and therefore what a human life can be.

I will not let the vision drift loose of its gate, because that drift is exactly how every earlier generation of this dream curdled into a broken promise. None of what follows arrives the morning a machine switches on. The burner fleet that delivers power at this price is years of hard engineering away, and one of its cheapest routes is gated — as the earlier chapters said plainly, and I will not now un-say it — on lunar helium-3 that does not yet exist at scale, which is precisely why the breeder, Hyperion, is designed to make helium-3 on Earth in the meantime, so the burner is never left staring at the sky, waiting. So read what follows as a map of consequences, not a set of promises. It is the answer to the only question that ever justified the decades of work and the honest years of doubt: if we succeed, what is it for?

This is what it is for.

The morning the water stopped being a wall

Begin where scarcity has drawn blood longest: at the tap. For most of history a city in a dry country woke to the oldest question there is — where does the water come from? — and the answer was a river it did not control or an aquifer it was mining toward empty. But the sea was always right there, the largest reservoir on the planet, and the only thing standing between the city and it was energy. The membranes work today; you can walk through the plants. The thermodynamic floor for pulling fresh water from seawater is a little over a kilowatt-hour a cubic metre, and real plants spend three or four. That gap — not chemistry, not the membrane, only the price of the electron pushed through it — was the whole wall.

Watch firmness take the wall down. A desalination plant is capital-intensive machinery that hates to stop; its membranes resent being cycled, and every idle hour is expensive metal depreciating for nothing. Pair it with an intermittent source and it waits three-quarters of the year for the sun. Pair it with a generator that runs by nature and it runs by nature too — and the match between a continuous process and a continuous generator is the entire reason the water comes out cheap. Below the forty-dollar line the arithmetic simply inverts: the machine that was too expensive to run without stopping becomes the cheapest source of water the city has, and the city stops rationing and simply chooses to drink the sea, the way it chooses to build a bridge.

And the choice ripples outward the moment it is made. The hot, hypersaline brine that plants once fought to dump becomes an ore body — lithium and magnesium worth separating now that the chemistry pays for itself. Ancient aquifers under northern India and the North China Plain and the American West, drained in decades that took millennia to fill, become batteries instead of graves, recharged with power that no longer has to be hoarded. Ultrapure water — the kind a semiconductor fab and an electrolyser cannot live without — turns abundant where it was precious. And a nation that can make its own fresh water from the ocean negotiates its shared rivers from security rather than desperation, turning a knife-edge over a fixed river into a solvable question of who builds which plant. Water was the first hostage scarcity ever took. A cheap, tireless electron is how you ransom it back.

The bread of the air, and the forge that stops smoking

Follow the water inland and it becomes food, because behind every calorie is a hidden river and, one step further back, a hidden current of energy. Roughly half the people alive were, in a strict and literal sense, grown from natural gas: the Haber–Bosch process marries nitrogen from the air to hydrogen cracked out of methane, and it feeds the world while quietly venting the carbon of that feeding into the sky. You cannot switch it off. You can only change what feeds it — and the change is a single swap at the very front of the line. Split the hydrogen from water with cheap firm electricity instead of from gas, and the century-old reaction that grows half the world's food stops being a fossil enterprise. The nitrogen is already free, four-fifths of every breath. The molecule that comes out is identical. Only its history is clean. The bread, from now on, is baked out of the air.

Now stand the same electrolyser beside the same generator and feed it the other way, and it becomes the hinge of an entire industrial civilisation learning to make things without fire. Iron ore is rust — iron locked to oxygen — and for three centuries we tore the two apart the only way we knew, with carbon: `2 Fe₂O₃ + 3 C → 4 Fe + 3 CO₂`, every tonne of iron trailing nearly two tonnes of carbon dioxide, a fourteenth of all human emissions printed into the sky straight out of the chemistry. There is another reductant that does the same job and leaves only water: hydrogen. Run `Fe₂O₃ + 3 H₂ → 2 Fe + 3 H₂O`, melt the sponge iron in an electric arc furnace, and steel walks out clean. The route is not speculative; direct-reduced iron is made at scale today. What kept it a demonstration was the hydrogen — and what turns green steel from a subsidised showpiece into the default way to make the metal that builds the fusion plants themselves is firm power, the kind that keeps the expensive electrolyser working every hour of the year rather than idling for the calm. The gate is real and I will name it: furnaces retrofitted, hydrogen stored and handled, none of it free. But the physics is settled. The only fire steel still needs is the one we choose to light.

And what happens to steel happens across the whole forge of the modern world, because nearly a third of global emissions come not from cars or homes but from the heat and chemistry of making things — and a factory needs its power unwavering, since a single flicker can scrap a whole shift at a fab that then takes days to recover. Cement gets clean process heat and affordable capture for the carbon the limestone itself exhales. Aluminium — electricity stored in metal — stops chasing the few places with cheap hydropower and settles wherever a compact generator can stand. The copper and lithium and rare earths a green economy runs on stop being mined by burning the very thing they are meant to replace. The recycled tonne quietly becomes the cheaper tonne, and the circular economy becomes plain economics rather than a virtue. Chemicals and plastics build themselves from captured carbon and split water, the petroleum left in the ground. One boiler, one furnace, one smelter at a time, the smoke goes out of the making of things — not by asking anyone to make less, but by changing what the making runs on.

The thinking machines, fed at last

There is a reason a fusion company and an artificial-intelligence company find themselves talking, and it is not fashion. Modern AI is, physically, a machine for turning electricity into inference, and it has run headlong into the wall that electricity is neither cheap, firm, nor clean enough to feed its appetite. A hyperscale computing campus does not breathe like a city; it does not dip at midnight or slump on a holiday. It draws the same vast power at three in the morning as at noon, every day of the year — a flat line on the chart, the purest firm load our economy has ever built. And the brutal arithmetic of serving a flat line is that the cheapest way to feed it is with a generator that produces one, not with a fluctuating source propped up on batteries sized for the worst still night of the year.

This is why one of the burner's housings, MetroVolt, is built to stand at the fence line of the campus rather than at the far end of a transmission corridor. In more and more regions the true limit on where computation can grow is no longer the chips or the capital but the interconnection queue — the years-long wait for permission to draw from a grid that has run out of room. A firm source behind the meter changes what "possible to build here" even means, and does it without lighting a molecule of gas. Freed from chasing the few places with spare capacity, the data centres scatter to where the users are, or the cold climates, or the industries they serve — and the great simulations ride the same abundance: climate, drug discovery, protein folding, materials, all limited today by how much compute a lab can afford. There is even a loop that closes on itself, and I find it quietly astonishing: this very machine was designed with large-scale simulation and AI surrogates, so cheaper computation makes the next hard machine easier to design than this one was.

And there is a deeper symmetry, the one that made the partnership inevitable. The machine that would feed the thinking machines is itself run by a thinking machine. The burner is a plasma at conditions no human reflex could govern; the control stack — built with NVIDIA, the one external partner named in this book — runs surrogate models up to three orders of magnitude faster than direct calculation, a magnet twin some seven-thousand-fold quicker, so a controller can rehearse the next microsecond before it arrives. Its fused early-warning system reads a coming disruption forty-seven milliseconds out where a single diagnostic would offer barely two and a half; a safety clamp caught every one of three thousand injected limit violations across a hundred and fifty thousand steps, with zero escapes. Artificial intelligence does not make the plasma burn hotter. It makes a burning plasma governable — which is the precondition under a generator you would dare to site beside the racks it powers. (I keep one discipline here against the field's habit: fusion needs no quantum computer, and quantum computing offers this work no near-decade advantage. What is genuinely near is quantum sensing for diagnostics — the honest part, named as such.)

The world set in motion

You do not put a reactor in a car, and I will not pretend otherwise. But nearly every clean way to move people and goods is, underneath, a way of delivering electricity — and its price and firmness decide whether the clean option wins. The reactor never leaves the ground; it just makes the electrons, the hydrogen, and the synthetic fuel cheap enough to finally choose over what we burn. The electric fleet charges heaviest at two in the morning, when the sun is long gone and the honest match is not another solar panel but a source that is simply there. Freight rail's long-life arithmetic tips off diesel the moment the price of power over the line stays low and firm. And for the machines too heavy, too far, too fast for a battery — the ship crossing an ocean, the aircraft eight miles up — the answer comes out of the air itself.

A wing cares about energy per kilogram, and aviation fuel packs some forty-three megajoules into every one, which is why long-haul flight will want a dense liquid for a long time yet. It can come out of the air. Take carbon dioxide — from a flue or from the sky — and hydrogen split from water, build them back into a synthetic crude, and refine a jet fuel chemically indistinguishable from the fossil kind, the carbon out of the engine only the carbon that went in. The chemistry is old and proven; its entire cost is the energy poured into making it. Drop that below the line and hold it there, and clean flight crosses from gesture to supply. The same chain, one branch earlier, fuels the sea:

A generator standing on land quietly decides what a ship burns in the middle of the ocean, and what an aircraft burns over the pole. Every hard-to-electrify machine we have waits at the end of a chain whose first link is a cheap, firm electron.

The healing

Not every gift of these machines is measured in megawatts. Some are measured in the vials a hospital's nuclear-medicine department opens each morning — and, wider by far, in the plain fact of reliable power where there has never been any. More lives turn on a clinic that keeps its lights and its refrigerators running than on any exotic isotope, and I will not let the rare thing crowd out the common one.

But the rare thing is real. The breeder makes its fuel by flooding its blanket with the hardest neutrons fusion throws — 14-MeV neutrons, with a breeding lever above one, replacing the fuel it burns — and a neutron source that prolific is, almost incidentally, one of the most useful instruments a country can own. The isotope behind tens of millions of cardiac and cancer scans a year hangs today on a handful of ageing reactors, any one of which going down for repairs can ripple into a shortage that reaches the patient on the table. Slide the right target into the flux and the same neutrons that breed tritium in one part of the blanket breed medicine in another. And helium-3 — which the breeder co-produces, and which the burner will one day crave from the Moon — is prized here and now for lung imaging and ultra-cold refrigeration, its supply eked today from the slow decay of aged warheads, which is no way to run a hospital. The fuel cycle, it turns out, has a bedside manner. Around all of it stands the humbler mercy: the clinic refrigerator that stops failing at the worst possible second, the operating theatre whose power does not flicker mid-procedure, the cold chain whose gap simply never opens — the hour that never goes dark.

The repair

Firm clean power is the largest single lever on emissions ever placed within human reach, because so much of what warms the planet is simply the burning of fuel for energy — and the largest, least glamorous move of all is switching one plant off and another on. Here most clean alternatives quietly fail the interview. A coal plant promises power on a windless week in January; a solar farm with a battery can chase that promise but cannot make it. Firmness is the whole difference between a substitute and a supplement. A firm, dispatchable, carbon-free generator does not sit beside the retiring coal plant hedged with batteries — it takes the plant's place on the grid diagram, same node, same reliability, same always-there, and simply stops emitting. Along the burner's roughly four-hundred-and-forty-metre axis, magnets reaching a peak near twenty-six and a half tesla in the end plugs hold a plasma in a bottle with no ends to leak through, burning deuterium and helium-3 at a neutron fraction near five and a half percent and converting much of its output directly rather than by boiling water — which is why it drinks so little cooling water and throws off so little waste heat. The point of all that engineering is one unremarkable virtue: it is there, all the time, on purpose. And that virtue is what finally lets a grid switch off the gas "peaker" kept breathing in reserve behind even the greenest-looking system — not overbuilt, not over-stored, not hedged. Replaced.

Then, beyond retiring the fire, the harder work: taking back some of what we already put up. Direct air capture pulls carbon dioxide straight out of an atmosphere where it is only four parts in ten thousand — un-mixing the sky, thermodynamically expensive, which is the single reason it has stayed a boutique affair measured in thousands of tonnes against a problem measured in billions. Firm fusion power attacks that expense from both sides: cheap enough to run continuously until the numbers close, and clean enough that the cure is not worse than the disease, because capture run on dirty power can emit more than it removes. I keep this one honest, because it is the item most easily oversold: direct air capture is not permission to keep emitting. It is the slow, expensive repair for what could not be prevented, and it earns its place only after the cheaper move — retiring the fossil plant — is made. But a warming world will need repair, and for the first time the medicine we always knew how to make becomes something we can also afford to take, at the dose the wound requires. Around it, the rest of the repair we long knew how to do and never could afford: degraded land greened with cheap desalinated water, legacy pollution finally cleaned rather than fenced off, carbon turned back into stone by enhanced weathering, and — the quiet giant — high-yield indoor farms and water drawn from the sea shrinking the human footprint so that abundance for people and room for everything else stop being a cruel trade.

The city, and the places that must never go dark

A city is a machine for concentrating people, and everything about it — water, heat, transport, air — is downstream of the price and reliability of energy. For the first time the generator can be a good neighbour rather than a distant, dirty thing kept over the horizon: built compact and clean, the burner can sit near the demand it serves, cutting the losses of long transmission and freeing the grid from a single fragile artery one storm can sever. It anchors a microgrid that "islands" — that disconnects from a failing grid and keeps a hospital or a water plant alive on the worst day of the year, not the average one. It electrifies the buildings whose heating peaks on the coldest, darkest winter nights, exactly when the sun is weakest and only firm winter power will do. And within a few years of the combustion leaving a city's power, heat, and transport, the air over it changes measurably — felt in every pair of lungs, the youngest and the oldest first.

And because firm power is, at bottom, a strategic asset, no honest telling would leave out the places someone is actively trying to switch off. One of the burner's housings — Aegis — is built for exactly this, and I state it plainly rather than tuck it away, because the same firmness that runs a data centre runs a base, and pretending otherwise would be an evasion I have promised not to commit. A base, a radar station, a command centre guarantees its power today with fields of diesel fed by slow, vulnerable convoys; a compact generator on site removes both the emissions and the ambush, because the convoy that never rolls cannot be attacked. The same logic hardens the civilian infrastructure — water, hospitals, communications — whose deepest shared vulnerability is the grid, turning one fragile network into many resilient cells that fail, if at all, one at a time rather than all at once in a cascade. And a nation that makes its own tritium, helium-3, and medical isotopes from a breeder it controls no longer has to ask anyone's permission for the scarce materials a modern state runs on. Development has always been rationed by the price of power. Lower it for everyone, and you widen the circle of who gets to build a future.

The circle widened

This is where cheap firm power does its most human work, and where I hold myself hardest to honesty, because infrastructure is slow and political and reaches the poorest last, whatever the physics allows and however fiercely I wish it otherwise. Strip away the machines and the vision, and energy poverty is simply poverty wearing another name. Something close to the population of a whole continent still lives without reliable electricity — not without any power, often, but without the boring, dependable, always-there kind you can build a life on top of.

So watch a region get it, because the transformation is made of small, unglamorous mercies compounding. The clinic's refrigerator stops failing, so the vaccines survive the hot end of the line. The pump runs when the field needs it, so the farmer plants for yield instead of gambling the whole season on the rains. The workshop keeps its full hours, and the wage that comes with them. A child reads after dark by a steady bulb instead of a kerosene lamp that is dim, dear, and dangerous to breathe beside — and the hours that adds to a childhood compound, over years, into something no aid programme can buy directly. Clean water arrives through the main rather than the pharmacy, and does more preventive medicine than any pill. The daily regressive tax the poorest pay — kerosene, charcoal, diesel when the grid is down, a premium charged simply for being poor — lifts. None of it asks for a scientific miracle. It asks for the humblest thing on this whole list: power that is simply, boringly, there.

And standing behind every one of these, the largest freedom of all and the quietest — the one the rest depend on. So much of geopolitics is a scramble over fuels: who holds them, who controls the routes, who can be cut off in a hard winter. Energy generated anywhere, from fuel drawn ultimately from seawater and the Moon, drains that poison from the well of international relations. I no longer write, as I once did, that this makes peace inevitable — that was a young writer's word, and honesty is the currency of this book. Abundant, siteable, everyone-can-make-it energy does not guarantee peace. It does something more modest and, in the long arc, more durable: it dissolves one of the oldest and most reliable reasons human beings have ever had to fight. It removes a driver. It does not remove them all — and the distance between those two sentences is the distance between a slogan and an argument I can defend without flinching.

The frontier

And then, because the same energy density that runs a city can run a world, the story leaves the ground — and here I keep the caveat at full strength, without apology, because almost all of this lies further off than everything above, and some of it very much further, past the edge of what this book can promise. The Moon keeps the most unforgiving schedule in the inner solar system: fourteen Earth-days of sunlight, then fourteen of dark. A solar panel is dead weight through every hour of that fortnight-long night, and it is this — more than radiation or dust or distance — that quietly kills most designs for a permanent base on the drawing board. A settlement either carries its own firm power across the dark or it freezes solid in it, and there is no third option and no one to call. A compact fusion generator runs straight through the long night: firmness in its most literal form, where the alternative to power is not inconvenience but death.

And once there is power to spare on the surface, the loop closes in a way I still find astonishing. Lunar regolith holds helium-3, salted in grain by grain over billions of years of solar wind, and baking it out means heating vast tonnages of soil — which takes energy, which the generator supplies. Fusion power helps mine the very fuel that fusion power runs on. I will say the gate one final time, plainly: this is an unproven, off-world programme near the bottom of the readiness ladder, the single gate on which the burner's cheapest power waits — which is exactly why the breeder matters so much here and now, making helium-3 on Earth while the Moon is still a plan on paper, so the burner is never left waiting on the sky. Beyond that lie the nearer frontiers I will name but not oversell — ice split into propellant so a mission need not haul every gram up from the bottom of Earth's gravity well, orbital depots kept cold, factories in free fall — and, at the very top of the speculation ladder, generations away and beyond anything in these pages, the dirtiest industry lifted off a living planet altogether, and a propulsion that could turn months of travel into weeks. Naming the distance is not the same as denying the direction. Fusion should not be the reason humanity goes to the Moon. It should be a patient customer of a cislunar economy built for other reasons — a demand signal specific enough to help pull the infrastructure into being, and disciplined enough to wait for it rather than pretend it is already there.

What the fire is for

I promised a map of consequences, not a set of promises, and I will end by keeping that promise about promises. Notice the kind of world I have just walked you through, because its character is the whole argument. Almost nothing in it required a scientific miracle or an undiscovered law. Desalination works today; it is merely priced out. So does direct air capture, green steel, clean hydrogen, indoor farming, electric everything — the science is largely done, sitting on the shelf, and the single dominant term keeping it there marked too expensive is the cost of a firm kilowatt-hour. And I have tried, scene by scene, to keep the exceptions in plain view: the frontiers genuinely far off, the places where firmness matters more than price, the ones where cheap energy helps only at the margins and human habit or politics or taste must do the rest.

So this was never a hundred separate bets scattered across a table. It is one bet, made a hundred times over — a single wager wearing a hundred faces. Push the price of firm, clean power below the line and hold it there, and the shelf comes down: not everywhere at once, not on any date I will promise you, but steadily, city after city and field after field, as the arithmetic quietly inverts. What separates this book from the one I first wrote in 2024 is not that I have grown more certain the vision is nice — anyone can love a vision. It is that I now have a closed, published design and an honest, staged plan for reaching the price, with every gate named out loud, including the ones that lie outside our control and behind which the cheapest power patiently waits.

Energy is water. Energy is food. Energy is health, and clean air, and computation, and the freedom not to have your survival held hostage by geography or the weather or the price of a distant barrel. Energy is freedom — and here were more than a hundred of its shapes, and behind every one of them the same quiet, stubborn, honest machine, doing the one thing that changes all the rest.

This chapter's forty. All three at once — Q40, $40 a megawatt-hour, within forty years — because every door in this brighter tomorrow opens only when the physics, the price, and the clock are kept together.
