Energy, Sovereignty, and the End of Resource War

Look at the night side of our planet from orbit and you are looking at a map of power in both senses of the word. The rivers of light tracing the coastlines and the great dark gulfs between them are not just where people live; they are where energy flows, and for the whole industrial age the flow of energy has been the deep current under the surface of history. I gave this chapter of the 2024 version a title I no longer defend without qualification: With Fusion Energy, World Peace Is Not Only Possible — It's Inevitable. I still believe the sentence points at something true. But "inevitable" is a young writer's word, and honesty is the whole currency of this book, so let me trade it for a claim I can actually stand behind and prove out over the length of a chapter. Abundant, siteable, everyone-can-make-it energy does not guarantee peace. It does something more modest and, in the long arc, more durable: it dissolves one of the oldest and most reliable reasons human beings have ever had to fight each other. It removes a driver. It does not remove them all — and the difference between those two statements is the difference between a slogan and an argument.

I did not come to this argument out of a textbook. I came to it from a childhood spent crossing the very lines it describes. I was born in India and raised across three countries — a stretch of years at school in England, summers that taught me to surf before I was eight, and, from the age of thirteen, a home in the United States — and a child who moves between worlds like that learns something a child who stays in one place can miss: that the gap between them is, more than anything else, a gap in power. Not political power — the literal kind, the kind measured in watts. I watched human beings no different in worth or wit live radically different lives on opposite sides of that gap, and the variable that tracked the difference most faithfully was never culture or character. It was how much energy a person could command, and at what price. I have spent my life since trying to close that gap at its source. This chapter is why I believe fusion can.

So this is the argument, and it reaches back to the first fire and forward to the first star we build on Earth. Conflict has many engines — belief, grievance, fear, ambition, memory. But underneath a great many of the wars that have actually been fought sits a stubbornly physical fact: the things a society runs on are not distributed evenly across the surface of the Earth, and the most concentrated of those things is energy. Energy is not merely one commodity among many. It is the master commodity, the one from which all the others are made. Fresh water at scale is desalinated with energy. Food at scale is fertilized, irrigated, harvested, and moved with energy. Metal is smelted, cities are cooled, armies move, and information is computed — all on energy. A civilization is, at bottom, a structure for capturing energy and spending it on everything it wants to be. Whoever controls the supply of it holds a lever over everyone who needs it, which is everyone. The central promise of the machines in this book is not only cheaper power. It is the quiet demolition of that lever — the removal of a fulcrum that has moved nations for a hundred and fifty years.

Scarcity is the raw material of coercion

Start with the mechanism, because the mechanism is what matters and the sentiment is easy. Why does energy, specifically, generate conflict so reliably? Not because it is valuable — many things are valuable. It is because the way we have made energy for the whole industrial era combines three properties that, together, read almost like a design specification for leverage. Nature did not intend it as a weapon. It became one anyway, the way any bottleneck does.

The first is geographic concentration. The richest deposits of the fuels that run the modern world are found in a small number of places, and those places were assigned not by need or by merit but by the geology of a few hundred million years ago — by where ancient sunlight, captured in ancient life, happened to be buried and pressed into carbon. A nation either sits on top of the deposit or it does not. If it does not, it must acquire the fuel from someone who does, on terms that someone else sets. That single accident of where the carbon settled has drawn more borders, funded more regimes, and justified more interventions than almost any idea a philosopher ever had.

The second is transport dependence. The deposit is rarely where the demand is, so the fuel must travel — by tanker across an ocean, by pipeline across a continent, through a canal, around a cape, down a strait narrow enough that a handful of ships could close it. The physical path the energy must take becomes as strategically precious as the deposit itself. A disproportionate share of the world's traded energy passes through a small number of maritime chokepoints, each one a place where the whole global supply could be pinched by whoever controls the water. A chokepoint is leverage you do not even have to own the oil to hold. You only have to sit astride the road.

The third is inelastic demand. A society cannot simply decide to need less energy the way it can decide to buy fewer luxuries. Energy demand is close to a need, not a want; when the price triples, the hospitals still need power and the trucks still need to run. That inelasticity is what turns a supply interruption from an inconvenience into an emergency, and an emergency is what makes coercion work. You cannot be coerced by the threatened loss of something you can live without.

None of these three properties is a moral failing of anyone in particular. That is the important and uncomfortable point. No villain designed the resource lever; it is an emergent property of physical facts — where the carbon settled, where the demand grew up, how much a society can do without. Decent people acting rationally inside a scarce, concentrated, transport-dependent energy system will still find themselves drawn into the logic of the lever, because the logic is structural rather than personal. A nation that would never choose to be coercive still cannot ignore that its lights depend on a strait it does not control, and so it builds a navy, or seeks an alliance, or props up a supplier, not out of malice but out of the ordinary arithmetic of not wanting to freeze in the dark. This is why exhortation has never solved it. You cannot moralize your way out of a structural condition. You can only change the structure — and changing the structure is a physics-and-industry problem, which is precisely the kind this book is about. That is the quiet radicalism of the whole enterprise: it treats a problem the world has always handed to diplomats and generals as, instead, a problem for engineers.

Put those three together and you have the anatomy of the resource lever. A concentrated supply, forced to travel a vulnerable path, feeding a demand that cannot be switched off. Whoever sits at any point along that chain — the deposit, the strait, the pipeline valve, the refinery — holds a grip on everyone downstream. That grip is not usually exercised as open war. Most of the time it is quieter: the embargo, the price shock used as a weapon, the pipeline turned down in winter, the alliance bought with a guaranteed cargo, the fleet stationed permanently to keep a sea lane open. But the whole apparatus of pressure, from the softest diplomatic squeeze to the outright seizure of an oilfield, rests on the same foundation. Scarcity is the raw material of coercion. Remove the scarcity and you have not passed a treaty, but you have taken away the material that the coercion was built from. You have not commanded the hand off the lever. You have removed the lever.

You cannot corner a market in something everyone can make. You cannot blockade a sea lane that no longer carries anything anyone needs.

A short history of the lever

The resource lever is older than oil, older than the internal-combustion engine, older than the word "geopolitics." It is worth walking through its history, because the shape of the thing becomes clear only when you watch it repeat — the same anatomy of concentration, transport, and inelastic need reassembling itself around each new fuel a civilization learns to burn. The fuels changed. The lever did not.

Consider the age of coal and rail, the first energy system dense enough to run an industrial society. A nation's power in that era could be read almost directly off its coal seams; the countries that rose fastest were, with few exceptions, the countries sitting on the richest and most accessible carbon. But even then the lever was not only about the deposit. A steam navy could reach only as far as it could refuel, so the strategic map of the last great maritime age was, in large part, a map of coaling stations — a chain of held ports and islands strung along the sea routes, each one a place where a fleet's range was quietly extended or, in a rival's hands, cut short. Command of the ocean was, at bottom, command of the bunkers along it. That is the lever in its earliest industrial form: not merely who owned the fuel, but who controlled the places it had to pass through to be useful.

Then came a decision that has echoed for a hundred years, made at the dawn of the last century by a great maritime power that chose to convert its battle fleet from coal it dug within its own borders to oil it would have to import from far away. The engineering case was overwhelming — oil gave more speed, more range, less labor, a faster return to sea. But the choice wrote dependence into the very heart of national defense. A navy that had run on domestic coal now ran on a fuel that had to be carried across oceans from distant fields, and from that single trade of security for performance flowed a century of foreign entanglement whose deep logic, underneath every stated justification, was the securing of a supply. Once a nation's power depends on a fuel it does not hold, it will reach — through alliance, through presence, through force when it judges it must — toward the places that fuel comes from. This is not a moral observation about any particular nation. It is the structural gravity of dependence, and every industrial power has felt its pull.

The century that followed was the century of the tanker and the pipeline, and it refined the lever into an instrument of exquisite precision. Vast quantities of the world's traded energy came to move by sea, which meant that a handful of narrow straits and canals — places where the great tanker routes pinch down to a few miles of water — acquired a strategic value out of all proportion to their size. A chokepoint is the purest expression of transport dependence: a spot on the map where a small force, or a single incident, can threaten the energy supply of nations that lie thousands of miles away and have no say in the matter. Whole navies have been sized, stationed, and justified by the need to keep such passages open. The pipeline added a slower, more surgical version of the same power: a valve that could be turned down in winter to remind a downstream customer of its dependence, an act of pressure that never fired a shot and never had to.

And running beneath all of it was the oldest instrument of all — the deposit itself as a source of patronage and coercion. A supplier could reward a friendly government with a guaranteed cargo and punish an unfriendly one by finding reasons to withhold it. Client relationships were built and dissolved on the promise of fuel. Interventions were mounted, at real cost in lives and treasure, to keep a well or a field in reliable hands. None of this required anyone to be uniquely wicked. It required only the structure I described a moment ago — a supply that is concentrated, that must travel, and that no one can do without — and ordinary human beings acting rationally inside that structure. The lever pulls the people who hold it as surely as it pulls the people it is used against.

I rehearse this history not to assign blame but to establish a pattern, because the pattern is the argument. Every time a civilization has changed its master fuel — wood to coal, coal to oil, oil toward gas and beyond — the lever has survived the transition by reassembling itself around the new fuel's own geography of scarcity. A cleaner fuel did not dissolve it. A more efficient fuel did not dissolve it. The only thing that could ever dissolve the lever is a fuel that breaks the pattern itself — that is not concentrated, does not have to travel, and cannot be monopolized. That is a very specific set of demands, and until now no energy source has met all three. The rest of this chapter is about one that can.

Three properties that pull the lever apart

The reason I think fusion is different in kind — not just a cleaner fuel but a change in the political physics of energy — is that the fuel cycle in this book attacks all three of those properties at once. It does not merely add another supplier to the existing map. It changes what the map is for. Imagine redrawing the world's energy geography not by moving the deposits but by making the very idea of a deposit obsolete. That is the move.

Abundance, against concentration. The primary fuel of the near-term machine, deuterium, is simply a heavy form of hydrogen, and it is in the water. Every ocean on Earth holds it, in a ratio that does not care about borders — the same enrichment in a hostile sea as in a friendly one, the same in a superpower's harbor as in a small nation's bay. There is no deuterium cartel and there cannot be one, because there is no deposit to seize — only seawater, which touches almost every nation and can be reached by the rest. The energy locked in that fuel is staggering out of all proportion to its bulk: a fusion fuel load is measured in kilograms where the fossil equivalent is measured in shiploads, so the amount any society needs is small and the source is everywhere. When the fuel is drawn from the global commons rather than dug from a few sovereign patches of ground, the first property of the lever — concentration — simply is not there to grip. The map goes flat.

Siteability, against transport dependence. A fusion generator makes its power where it stands. It does not sit at the end of a pipeline or a sea lane that must be held open by force; its fuel is a cabinet of material replenished at intervals measured in years, not a river of hydrocarbons that must flow continuously or the lights go out. When power is made at the point of use, the strategic value of the chokepoint evaporates. The strait is still a strait, but it no longer holds a knife to anyone's throat, because the thing it used to throttle no longer has to pass through it. A great deal of the world's naval posture, its permanent deployments, its standing anxieties, exists to keep energy moving along vulnerable paths — an enormous, ceaseless, expensive act of holding a door open. Energy that does not have to move cannot be interdicted, and a path that carries nothing vital is a path no one has to fight for. The door can simply be let go.

Reproducibility, against monopoly. This is the property I care about most, and it is the one that distinguishes an honest energy transition from the mere transfer of a monopoly to new hands. The physics of these machines is published — open-access, peer-reviewed, reproducible by any competent team with the will and the industrial base to build it. The design is not a secret to be hoarded; the granted patent that anchors it is a public document, which is what a patent is. The point of publishing is precisely that the knowledge cannot be re-concentrated. A future in which one nation cracks fusion and keeps it locked away would simply rebuild the old lever with a new fuel — a new cartel, a new dependency, a new set of clients kept in line by the threat of being cut off. Publication is the deliberate refusal of that future. When the recipe is in the open, energy sovereignty stops being a prize a few can win and becomes a capability many can build. That is not a loss of advantage. It is the difference between selling power and dissolving the very grounds for extortion — between owning the fire and teaching the world to make it.

The instruments of pressure, and their undoing

It helps to be concrete about how the lever is actually pulled, because "energy coercion" is an abstraction and the abstraction hides how varied and how routine the reality is. Open war over a fuel is the rarest and crudest form. Most of the pressure is quieter, and it is worth cataloguing the instruments — not to alarm, but because each one has a specific structural weakness that a fuel everyone can make happens to strike directly.

The first instrument is the embargo: the refusal to sell, or the refusal to let others sell, to a targeted party. Its power is entirely a function of concentration. An embargo bites only when the target cannot readily get the same thing elsewhere; the fewer the alternative suppliers, the deeper the bite. Against a fuel drawn from every ocean on Earth, the embargo simply has nothing to grip. You cannot refuse to sell a nation the deuterium in its own coastal water. The instrument does not fail so much as it ceases to describe anything real.

The second is the price weapon: not cutting supply off but manipulating its cost, flooding a market to ruin a rival producer or throttling it to inflict a shock on importers. This works because demand is inelastic — because a society that cannot quickly reduce how much energy it needs must pay whatever the price becomes. Firm fusion power made at the point of use changes the arithmetic underneath the weapon. When a large and growing share of a nation's energy comes from machines whose fuel is cheap, abundant, and stockpiled on site in quantities measured in years, a price spike in the old traded fuels no longer holds the same knife to the economy's throat. The demand that made the weapon work has been given somewhere else to go.

The third is the valve: the pipeline turned down, the cargo delayed, the maintenance that stretches suspiciously long during a cold season. This is transport dependence weaponized — it works only because the energy must flow continuously along a path the coercer controls. Power generated where it is consumed has no valve to turn. Its fuel arrives not as a continuous river that can be dammed but as a periodic delivery of a small mass of material, and between deliveries the machine runs on what it already holds. You cannot throttle a supply that is not, at the moment of pressure, in transit.

The fourth is the chokepoint itself — the threat, latent or open, to close a strait or a sea lane through which a rival's energy must pass. I have described why this evaporates: energy that does not have to travel cannot be interdicted, and a passage that no longer carries anything vital is no longer worth the fleet that guards it or the force that would seize it. The chokepoint remains a place on the map. It stops being a lever.

The fifth and subtlest is patronage — supply as a reward and its withdrawal as a punishment, the building of client relationships on the promise of guaranteed fuel. This one is undone not by any single property but by all of them together, because patronage depends on the client having no other option. A nation that can make its own energy from its own water, on a published design it did not have to buy, is a nation with nothing to be a client about. It cannot be bought with a cargo it does not need, nor disciplined by the threat of losing one.

Notice the common thread. Every instrument on that list is a way of exploiting one of the three structural properties — concentration, transport dependence, inelastic need — and every one of them is disarmed not by good intentions or better treaties but by an energy source that lacks the property the instrument feeds on. This is why I keep insisting the peace argument is a physics-and-industry argument rather than a diplomatic one. You do not talk a coercer out of the lever. You build the thing that removes what the lever was made of, and then the instruments, one by one, stop working — not because anyone agreed to set them down, but because there is nothing left for them to grip.

What "sovereignty" means when everyone can have it

There is an apparent paradox here that is worth facing directly, because it is where the argument earns its keep. Elsewhere in this book I argue hard for sovereignty — for a nation building its own fusion industry, its own materials supply chain, its own fuel, on ground it holds. How do I square that with a chapter arguing that fusion dissolves the leverage of energy? Isn't sovereignty just leverage by another name?

It is not, and the distinction is the whole point. The old energy order was rivalrous: because the supply was concentrated, one nation's security was purchased at the cost of another's dependence. My access meant your exposure. Sovereignty in that world was a zero-sum grip, and the grip was the weapon. The energy order fusion makes possible is non-rivalrous in exactly the property that matters: my building a fusion industry does not deplete a shared deposit, does not close a shared sea lane, does not make your fuel scarcer or your position weaker. The fuel is in your ocean too. The design is in your hands too. When sovereignty is something every nation can achieve simultaneously, without any of them having to take it from another, it stops being a lever and becomes a floor. It is the difference between a fortress that dominates the valley and a house that shelters its own family — both are secure, but only one of them threatens the neighbors. The whole grim game changes character when the prize can be won by everyone at once, because a prize that everyone can hold is no longer worth fighting anyone for.

That is the honest reconciliation. I want every nation to hold its own energy, because a nation that holds its own energy cannot be coerced through it — and a world of nations that cannot be coerced through energy is a world with one of its oldest quarrels defused. Universal sovereignty is not a contradiction of peace. It is a mechanism for it. You do not get peace by making everyone dependent on a benevolent supplier; that just relocates the lever and hopes the hand on it stays kind. You get peace by making the lever unnecessary — by ensuring that no one needs to grip anyone else's supply, because their own is secure and no one else's is scarce.

Peace built on a kind monopolist lasts exactly as long as the kindness. Peace built on universal access to the fuel does not depend on anyone's good behavior.

Firmness is a security property

There is a dimension of the argument I have so far left implicit, and it matters enough to make explicit, because without it the promise of "abundant energy" can quietly reintroduce the very vulnerability it claims to remove. Abundance is necessary but not sufficient. The energy that dissolves the lever must not only be plentiful and sited where it is used — it must be firm. It must be there when it is called for, on demand, through the night and through the still, windless week, without asking the weather's permission. Firmness is not a comfort feature. It is a security property, and it is where several otherwise admirable clean-energy visions quietly hand the lever back.

Consider what happens when a society leans hard on energy sources that are abundant and domestic but intermittent — that produce splendidly when the sun is high or the wind is strong and not at all otherwise. Such a grid is genuinely freed from the fuel deposit, which is real progress. But to keep the lights on across the dark and the calm it must either store enormous quantities of energy or keep a fleet of dispatchable backup ready to fire the moment the weather turns. If that storage depends on a narrow set of critical minerals mined in a few places, or that backup runs on an imported fuel, then the lever has not been removed. It has only been moved — from the primary supply to the materials of storage, or to the backup fuel that covers the gaps. A dependence disguised as independence is still a dependence, and an adversary looking for leverage will find it wherever the firmness actually comes from.

This is precisely why the machine that carries the peace argument in this book is a firm generator rather than an intermittent one. The burner — whose engineering fills the chapters around this one — is designed to produce steady, dispatchable, baseload power, the kind a hospital or a foundry or a city can lean its whole weight against. It does not wait for the wind. A society powered by such machines is not merely drawing its fuel from a global commons; it is drawing firm power from that commons, which means the abundance actually translates into the one thing security requires — energy you can count on at the moment you need it, with no gap for a coercer to exploit.

I have to be honest here about the staging, because the honesty is the point of this book and firmness is not free or instant. The path to that firm burner runs through a first machine — the breeder, Hyperion — that makes no net electricity at all, because it is not a power plant but a factory for the fuel and materials the firm burners will run on; the schedule that gets from that foundry to a commercial firm fleet is laid out in detail elsewhere in this book. The firmness that de-weaponizes energy is the endpoint of that staircase, not its first step, and I will not pretend the staircase can be skipped. But notice that even the staging serves the sovereignty argument: a nation that makes its own fuel and burns it in its own firm generators controls the whole chain, from the water intake to the wire, with no imported link for anyone to squeeze.

The firmness argument also explains the shape of the two housings the burner is built into, because security is not an abstraction — it is a specific site that must not go dark. One housing, the hardened fixed-site design I call Aegis, exists to keep a critical installation alive when the grid has failed and the fuel convoy has not arrived and the sea lane is closed — the exact conditions under which the old energy order turned a supply interruption into a catastrophe. The other, the data-center housing I call MetroVolt, answers the newest and most voracious form of inelastic demand there is: the computation that increasingly runs a civilization's economy, its science, and its defense, whose appetite for firm power is close to bottomless and cannot be met by a source that goes quiet at dusk. In both cases the point is the same. Firm power, made on the spot, from fuel you cannot be denied, is the physical form that energy security actually takes. Abundance opens the door; firmness is what lets you stop guarding it.

The off-world supply, and why it cannot be blockaded

There is a further piece to the fuel story that carries a peculiar strategic weight, and I introduce it here only in outline because a later chapter is devoted to it. The scale version of the burner — the fleet that turns fusion from a demonstrated machine into a civilization's power supply — reaches ultimately for helium-3, which is vanishingly rare on Earth and abundant in the lunar regolith, laid down grain by grain over billions of years by the solar wind. Think of the Moon as a vast, patient collector that has been trickling in a fuel our physics can burn since before there were oceans on Earth to hold the deuterium. I make no pretense that this is a near-term fact; it sits around 2040 in the plan this book keeps returning to, and I will not let it borrow credibility it has not yet earned.

But consider its character, because it is genuinely new under the sun. A fuel supply on the surface of the Moon is not on any nation's sovereign territory. It cannot be blockaded by a navy, because there is no sea lane to close. It cannot be seized by occupying a patch of desert, because the patch is a quarter of a million miles away and belongs, by the plainest reading of the treaties that govern such things, to no one and to everyone. A resource that lies beyond the reach of the ordinary instruments of terrestrial coercion is a resource that cannot easily be turned into a lever at all. For the entire history of our species, the source of a civilization's power has been something you could stand on, fence, and defend. This would be the first time the tap sits somewhere no border can be drawn around it. I do not want to romanticize this — access to space is itself unequal, and a naïve telling could simply move the old game to a new board. But the physical facts are genuinely different, and they push, on balance, away from the concentration that made the old fuel a weapon.

The resource curse, and its reversal

There is a cruel irony that students of political economy have documented for the better part of a century, and it belongs in any honest account of what energy does to a society from the inside. A nation that strikes a great deposit of the master commodity — that finds itself sitting atop a fortune in extractable energy — does not, on average, flourish the way intuition insists it should. Often it does the reverse. The windfall that ought to have built schools and clinics and a broad, durable prosperity instead hollows the country out, concentrates its power in a few hands, corrodes the institutions that were supposed to check that power, and leaves the majority of its people no better off, and sometimes poorer, than neighbors who were never so lucky. The pattern is common enough to have earned a name — the resource curse, the paradox of plenty — and, like the lever itself, its engine is structural rather than a matter of anyone's private wickedness. Good people inherit the curse along with the deposit.

Walk through why it happens, because the mechanism is what a manufactured fuel actually changes. It begins with rent. Wealth that comes from a single point in the ground — a field, a seam, a well — is captured, not earned across a whole society. Whoever controls that one geological chokepoint controls the revenue that flows from it, and controls it without needing the broad participation, the consent, or even the productivity of the population at large. The money does not arise from millions of people making and trading and building; it wells up from one place, and power pools around the tap. A society's center of gravity shifts from its people to its wellhead.

From that single fact the other distortions follow. A government funded by broadly taxing its citizens must, in some measure, answer to them; the need to raise revenue from the many is a leash, and representation has historically been the price a state pays for the right to tax. A government funded instead by a resource rent has slipped that leash. It need not ask the governed for anything, because it does not depend on them for its income; it can purchase their quiescence with a share of the rent, or compel it, and either way the ordinary accountability that scarcity of funds imposes on power simply does not form. The contract between state and citizen, which elsewhere is written in taxation, goes unsigned.

Meanwhile the single export hollows out everything around it. When one commodity dominates a nation's earnings, it warps the whole economy toward itself — bidding up the currency, drawing capital and talent away from the harder, slower work of building a diversified productive base, until the industries that make a society resilient wither for want of the attention that all flows to the rent. And there is a final turn of the screw: a concentrated, capturable source of wealth is precisely what a faction within, or an envious power without, will fight to seize. Concentration corrodes and concentration invites — the same property, working in both directions at once.

Now ask what changes when energy is manufactured rather than found — when it is made, distributed, and cheap instead of extracted from a few sovereign patches of ground. The whole apparatus of the curse loses its foundation, because the foundation was the single capturable point, and a manufactured fuel has none. There is no wellhead to stand on. The value of a fusion economy does not pool in a hole in the ground; it is spread across the machines that make the power and the many hands that build, run, and maintain them. You cannot seize the source of the wealth, because the source is an industrial capability and a design in the open, not a deposit with a fence around it. The rent that fed the rentier state has nowhere to collect.

That single structural difference propagates outward the way the curse did, but in the opposite direction. Wealth that comes from manufacturing rather than extraction comes from doing rather than holding — from skilled labor, from supply chains, from the diversified productive activity of a great many people, which is exactly the kind of base a government must engage and serve rather than sideline. Manufacturing rewards the broad participation that a rent renders unnecessary. And manufacturing behaves differently over time: a deposit depletes and grows dearer as it empties, but a manufactured good grows cheaper as a society makes more of it and learns the making — which is why the price on the spine of this book, the $40 per megawatt-hour, is a number won by scale and industrial learning rather than handed down by lucky geology. Extraction runs down. Manufacture runs down a learning curve. They are opposite motions, and they build opposite kinds of societies.

Notice how cleanly this maps onto the two-machine staging the rest of the book is built around. The breeder, Hyperion, is not a mine; it is a factory, whose near-term product is deliberately materials rather than electricity — an industrial plant that makes fuel and neutron-worked materials with skilled labor and a real supply chain behind it. The burner fleet that follows is distributed generation, power made in many places rather than pumped from one. Neither is an extractive rent. Both are the kind of broad, buildable, teachable industry whose wealth resists pooling around a single tap. When the recipe is published and the feedstock is in every ocean, the capability itself spreads rather than concentrates, and a capability that spreads cannot easily be cursed.

I will not overstate this, because the honesty is the point. Manufacturing can be concentrated too; a nation could try to hoard the machines, or the specialized materials that go into them, and rebuild a rent around the means of production rather than the fuel. The gradient points away from that — publication and an abundant feedstock are precisely the forces that resist re-concentration — but a gradient is not a guarantee, and a society that has organized its whole political order around a single extracted export will not shed that arrangement overnight simply because a better fuel exists. The transition away from the curse is its own long, difficult problem. What I am claiming is narrower and, I think, sound: the deep structural cause of the curse is the single capturable point, and a manufactured, distributed, reproducible energy source removes that point. It does not lift the curse by decree. It removes the thing the curse was built on.

A country cannot be cursed by a deposit it does not depend on — and there is no deposit to depend on when the fuel is made, not found.

Power as the precondition for dignity

I have argued this chapter almost entirely at the scale of nations — levers, straits, cartels, the grand machinery of coercion between states. But the deepest case for abundant energy is made at a smaller and more human scale, and it would be a failure of nerve to end without it. Underneath the geopolitics sits a plainer truth: reliable power is a precondition for human dignity, and its absence is one of the quietest, most grinding forms of deprivation there is. This is a different argument from the water chapter and from the catalogue of a hundred uses elsewhere in the book. It is not about what energy produces. It is about what its mere reliable presence permits — the floor of decency below which a life becomes a daily emergency.

Consider what a steady watt actually buys a person who has never had one. It buys light after dark, which is time itself — hours returned to a child for study and to an adult for work that the setting sun used to confiscate. It buys refrigeration, which is the difference between medicine that keeps and medicine that spoils, between a vaccine that survives the journey and one that dies on the way. It buys a pump, and a pump is clean water lifted from a well without a half-day's walk to carry it. It buys the cold chain, the clinic's night lighting, the charged phone that is a family's only link to a wage or a warning. None of this is luxury. It is the substrate of an ordinary life, and a great many human beings still live without it — not because the energy does not exist, but because the way we have made it could not be got to where they are at a price they could pay. Energy poverty is, at bottom, a delivery failure of the extractive, transport-dependent system this whole chapter has been dismantling.

That is where the peculiar shape of these machines matters most, and where the humanitarian case and the sovereignty case turn out to be the same case seen from below. A generator that makes its power where it stands, on fuel it can hold for years, is exactly the tool for the places the old grid could never reach and the places where the grid has been destroyed. Think of a settlement of the displaced — a camp that was meant to last a season and has lasted a decade, far from any transmission line, its clinics and water pumps and lights today kept alive, when they are kept alive at all, by convoys of diesel that must run a gauntlet of distance, cost, and sometimes gunfire to arrive. A firm, sited generator answers that need directly: power that does not depend on a fuel truck getting through, in a place where the fuel truck is the single most fragile link in the chain of survival. The same hardened, self-sufficient design I described earlier as keeping a critical defense site alive when the convoy fails is, turned to another purpose, the design that keeps a field hospital alive under the same failure. Firmness is a security property for a nation and a dignity property for a family, and it is the identical engineering.

The logic extends to the aftermath of catastrophe. When an earthquake or a storm or a war takes down a region's grid, the cruelest interval is the one between the disaster and the restoration — the weeks or months when the wires are down and the hospitals run on whatever fuel can be flown or trucked in against a collapsing road network. A generator whose fuel is a cabinet of material already on site, replenished on a scale of years rather than days, is a source of power that a disaster cannot easily sever, because there is no continuous supply line left exposed for the disaster to cut. Energy you cannot be denied, I have said, is energy no one can use against you; it is also energy the rubble cannot bury and the flood cannot wash out.

And here the humanitarian thread rejoins the argument this chapter opened with, because desperation and conflict are not strangers. Reliable power does not make a person peaceful, but its absence is a reliable ingredient of the conditions in which conflict breeds. Where there is no light, no cold chain, no pumped water, no way to run the small enterprise that lifts a family — where the days are a scramble at the edge of survival — there is exactly the soil in which grievance takes root and the recruiters of violence do their patient work. Idle, powerless, hopeless: those three words have described the antechamber of a great many conflicts. Energy abundance does not reach into a human heart and remove the capacity for hatred. What it can do is drain some of the desperation that hatred feeds on — give a young person a lit room, a charged connection to the wider world, a reason to build rather than to burn. That is not the whole of peace. But it is the same move as the rest of the chapter, made one life at a time: remove a driver.

I owe the reader the honest caveat, and it is the same one the whole book insists on. Abundance removes a driver of conflict; it does not remove conflict, and it does not, by itself, feed a hungry region or govern it justly or heal what war has torn. A generator is not a government. Power delivered into a place with no institutions to steward it can be captured like anything else, and dignity requires far more than watts — it requires the rule of law, the roof, the harvest, the peace to enjoy them. I am not claiming these machines end suffering. I am claiming something I can actually defend: that reliable, sited, deniable power is a precondition for a great deal of human dignity, one that hundreds of millions have been forced to live without, and that a fuel drawn from every ocean and burned in a machine that needs no convoy makes that precondition, for the first time, deliverable to almost anywhere a person is. And I owe one more piece of honesty, about the staging: this payoff lives with the burner, not the breeder, which means it lives downstream of the fuel factory and the first test machines rather than at the front of the schedule. The camp generator and the field hospital are a promise the later machine keeps, not a claim I can cash today. But it is a promise the physics permits, and permitting it is the reason to build.

Reliable power is not the whole of dignity. But it is the floor, and for the first time we can pour that floor almost anywhere a person stands.

The measured claim — what fusion does not do

Now the part the 2024 title skipped, and the part that makes the rest of the chapter trustworthy. Energy abundance is not a universal solvent for human conflict, and I would be selling you the same overreach I criticized in others if I implied it were. Wonder is worth nothing here without the receipt, and the receipt in this section is a subtraction.

Wars are not fought only over fuel. They are fought over ideology, over territory that means more than its resources, over ethnicity and religion and language, over historical grievance, over the ambitions of individuals, over sheer fear of a neighbor's intentions. Abundant energy does nothing directly to dissolve a border dispute rooted in memory, or an ethnic hatred cultivated over generations, or the ambition of a ruler who wants to be remembered as a conqueror. A world with limitless clean power could still be a world at war. I will not pretend otherwise, and any vision that pretends otherwise deserves your suspicion.

And even within the resource category, energy is only the deepest layer, not the only one. Fresh water is contested along shared rivers; arable land is contested as the climate shifts; the specific critical minerals that go into advanced technology — including, I note with some irony, the materials these very machines are built from — carry concentration problems of their own. Abundant energy touches several of these downstream: cheap power makes desalination a real answer to water scarcity, makes recycling and lower-grade ore economic, makes synthetic substitutes viable where a scarce input once forced a fight. Energy is the master commodity, so relieving its scarcity relieves pressure across the whole system — the way lowering the fever eases a dozen symptoms without curing every underlying disease. But "relieves pressure" is not "eliminates conflict," and the distance between those phrases is where honesty lives.

So here is the claim, stated as precisely as I can make it. Fusion of the kind this book describes — abundant in fuel, sited where power is used, and reproducible by design rather than hoarded — removes one of the largest and most persistent structural drivers of conflict between nations. It does not remove the others. It takes a lever that has bent history for a century and a half and makes it harder to grip, then harder to find, then finally not worth reaching for. That is a smaller claim than "world peace is inevitable." It is also a claim I can defend without flinching, which is the only kind worth making.

Why the drivers matter more than the incidents

Let me close by defending why removing a driver is worth a chapter even though it does not promise the end of all war. There is a difference between the sparks and the tinder, and civilizations rise and fall on that difference. Any particular conflict has its spark — an assassination, a border incident, a provocation, an insult. Sparks are unpredictable and probably ineradicable; human beings will always find reasons to strike the flint. But sparks land in a landscape, and the landscape can be dry or wet. Energy scarcity has been, for the whole industrial era, a vast field of dry tinder — a standing condition that turned local sparks into regional fires and gave great powers a permanent, material reason to involve themselves in distant quarrels. You cannot stop every spark. You can, over decades, soak the field.

That is the honest shape of the peace argument, and it is why it belongs at the front of this chapter rather than the triumphant back. I am not promising that the machines in this book will make humanity gentle. I am arguing something narrower and, I think, more credible: that they attack the conditions under which our ugliest impulses have most reliably turned into war. Take away the concentration, and the deposit is no longer worth seizing. Take away the transport dependence, and the sea lane is no longer worth a fleet. Take away the monopoly, and the supply is no longer worth a client state. What remains, in the space where the resource war used to be, is not automatically peace. But it is the absence of a reason — and history is largely the story of what people do with their reasons.

The rest of this chapter is about the machines that make that absence real: the hardened generator that keeps a critical site alive when the grid and the convoy have both failed, and the long, unglamorous industrial work of building the whole thing in a way that leaves the capability in many hands rather than few. Both are, at bottom, the same argument as this one, expressed in steel. A hundred generations of our ancestors fought, in part, over who would hold the fire. We are the first with a real chance to hand everyone their own. Energy you cannot be denied is energy no one can use against you. That is not the whole of peace. But it is a foundation you can pour, and it is time we started pouring it.
