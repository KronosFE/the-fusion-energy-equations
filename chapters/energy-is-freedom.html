Energy Is Freedom

Look up on a clear night and you are looking at the only power plants that have ever really mattered. Every star is a furnace fusing the lightest elements into heavier ones, flooding the dark with the energy that warms planets and, on at least one of them, woke matter into wondering about itself. We are, quite literally, the ash of that fire — assembled from atoms forged in stellar cores, running on sunlight banked in food and buried in coal. The whole human adventure has been a slow, stubborn campaign to bring a piece of that stellar fire down to Earth and put it to work. This book is about the moment that campaign stops being a dream and becomes an equation you can check.

Begin with the deepest truth I know about civilization, and it is not a poetic one — it is an engineering one. Every civilization is a machine for turning energy into everything else. Food is energy. Clean water is energy. Medicine, transport, light after dark, a message across an ocean, a mind augmented by a machine that thinks — all of it is energy, wearing different clothes. Give a people more energy, cheaper and cleaner, and you have not given them a commodity. You have given them options. You have given them freedom.

This is not a metaphor. It is the most reliable pattern in human history. When the price of energy falls, the ceiling on human possibility rises. The steam engine did not just pump water out of coal mines; it ended the age in which muscle — human and animal, and too often enslaved — was the only engine of work. Cheap electricity did not just light bulbs; it built the middle class. Every leap in human dignity has ridden on a leap in available energy. Run the film of the last ten thousand years and you will see one variable driving nearly all the others.

And every failure of dignity, if you follow it far enough, is a failure of energy. Wars are fought over the stuff that comes out of the ground. Water is rationed because desalination costs too much power. The poorest places on Earth are, almost without exception, the places where energy is scarcest and most expensive. We do not have an inequality problem that is separate from our energy problem. They are the same problem, and they have the same solution: make clean power so abundant and so cheap that scarcity stops being the organizing fact of human life.

Give a people more energy, cheaper and cleaner, and you have not given them a commodity. You have given them freedom.

The ladder we have been climbing for a million years

Here is a way to see the entire human story in a single quantity. Forget kings and borders for a moment and watch just one number: how much energy an ordinary person can command, and at what price. That number is the true measure of an age. Every rung on that ladder was a rung on the ladder of freedom, and the ladder has been under construction for a million years.

Start at the bottom, where we all began. A human body, working hard all day, produces about as much sustained power as a bright household bulb draws — roughly a hundred watts. Sit with that. For most of our species' existence, one hundred watts per person was the entire energy budget of the world. Everything a family ate, built, carried, and defended came out of the metabolic output of the people in it, supplemented, once we learned the trick, by fire. Fire was the first energy revolution, and notice what it bought: cooked food unlocked calories our raw diet had kept locked away, warmth pushed the habitable map north into whole continents, and light stole a few hours back from the night. More energy, more options. The pattern was set before we had cities — before we had writing to record it.

Agriculture was the next rung, and it is worth seeing clearly for what it truly was: humanity's first act of harvesting the Sun. A field of grain is a solar collector run through the chemistry of a seed, and farming let a village bank far more of the sun's energy than foraging ever could. That surplus is the hinge on which everything turns. It is what freed a fraction of people from growing food — to build, to write, to govern, to invent, to look at the stars and start doing arithmetic on them. Civilization is what a society does with its energy surplus. It always has been.

Then came the great multipliers of muscle. The ox and the harnessed horse let one person direct the power of an animal many times their own strength; a small design change like the padded horse collar, which let a horse pull without choking, quietly raised the output of every farm that adopted it. The waterwheel and the windmill went further still — they were the first engines that ran on no muscle at all, grinding grain and sawing timber and pumping water from something as free as a river or a breeze. A village with a mill was a village whose people no longer spent their days grinding grain by hand. That is not a small thing. That is hours of human life handed back, every single day, to be spent on something other than survival.

But water and wind are tied to place and weather, and muscle is tied to feeding the creature that supplies it. The ceiling did not truly lift until we learned to burn the buried sunlight of ancient forests — energy captured by plants a hundred million years ago and pressed into coal and oil, a savings account of solar power with a deep geological interest. Coal and the steam engine broke the link between work and location: for the first time, concentrated power could be summoned anywhere, at any hour, in any weather, in whatever quantity the coal supply allowed. Factories left the riverbanks. Ships stopped waiting on the wind. And then oil — denser, liquid, poured into a tank — put that power on wheels and wings and made the whole planet reachable in a single human lifetime. In a few generations we went from walking pace to escape velocity.

Electricity was the rung that changed daily life most intimately, because it made energy fungible. A single grid could take power from a distant dam or furnace and deliver it, clean at the point of use, to a lamp, a motor, a hospital, a machine that refrigerates food or dialyzes blood. Refrigeration alone rewrote human health by keeping food and medicine from spoiling. The electric motor did to the workshop what the steam engine had done to the nation. And in our own era, the same electron that lights a room now runs the computation that is reshaping science and medicine and the very way we think — the newest, and by far the hungriest, use we have ever invented for power.

Look at the whole climb from a great height and the lesson is unmistakable. Every rung added energy that was cheaper, cleaner at the point of use, more abundant, or more portable than the rung below — and every rung, without exception, widened the range of things a human being was free to do. We have never once regretted having more energy. We have only ever regretted where some of it came from. The next rung, the one this book is about, is the first that promises more energy and fixes where it comes from — the fire of the stars, made on the ground, from the most common elements in the universe.

The geopolitics of what comes out of the ground

Here is the shadow side of that shining history, and no honest book can skip it. When the energy a civilization depends on is buried in specific places, whoever sits on those places holds a lever over everyone who does not. Scarcity is not just an economic condition; it is a political one — perhaps the political one. Much of the last century's conflict, stripped of its slogans and its flags, was an argument over access to fuel: who controls the fields, who controls the routes, who can be squeezed by having a supply cut off in the dead of winter.

I want to be careful here, and non-partisan, because this is not a book about any nation's grievances. The point is structural, not tribal. A fuel that must be extracted from a handful of geologically lucky regions creates dependence by its very nature. Nations that have it can use it as a weapon; nations that lack it must buy it, guard the sea lanes it travels, and shape their foreign policy around never being cut off. Whole alliances have been built, and whole wars justified, on that single vulnerability. When your lights and your factories and your winters depend on a substance controlled by someone who may not wish you well, you are not fully free. Your sovereignty has an asterisk.

Abundance dissolves that leverage — and this is the quiet geopolitical promise buried underneath the engineering in this book, the reason its stakes are civilizational and not merely commercial. Imagine a fuel cycle that begins with the most common elements in the universe, and a power source that can be sited where the demand is rather than where the geology happens to be. Such a source takes the lever out of everyone's hands at once. You cannot embargo a resource that everyone can make. You cannot blockade a supply line to a plant that sits next to the city it powers. The prize at the end of cheap, firm, clean energy is not only lower bills and cleaner air. It is a century in which fewer nations have to fight over what comes out of the ground, because less and less of what matters comes out of the ground at all. That is not a footnote to the physics. For much of the human race, it may turn out to be the whole point.

The appetite that changes everything

For most of the last century, the rich world's electricity demand grew slowly and predictably — so slowly that planners could treat next year as a small edit of this year. That long, sleepy era is over. Something new has been born onto the grid, and it is pulling harder than anything in the history of electricity.

Artificial intelligence runs on electricity. Not a little — an astonishing amount. Training and running the models now woven through medicine, science, finance, and daily life consumes power at a scale that has caught entire national grids off guard. A facility that a decade ago drew what a town might use now draws what a city uses, and operators are planning the next generation in units that would have been an entire regional load not long ago. We have, in effect, invented a new kind of mind, and it eats electrons. The curve of its appetite is bending upward, not down, and it is bending faster than any demand curve utilities were ever built to absorb.

Understand why this collides so badly with the grid we have, because the mismatch is the whole point. Our power system was designed around demand that is diffuse, slow to change, and forgiving. Millions of homes and businesses, spread across a wide area, whose collective appetite rises and falls on a smooth and predictable daily rhythm. The new load is the opposite on every axis. It is enormous, it is concentrated in single locations, it arrives on the timescale of a construction project rather than a decade of planning, and — this is the part that breaks the old playbook — it wants to run flat out, around the clock, every hour of every day. Computation does not sleep at night the way a city does. It does not slow on weekends. It asks for its full draw at three in the morning in January, and it expects the power to simply be there.

That combination — vast, concentrated, sudden, and unrelentingly constant — is exactly the demand our grid is worst at serving. Building new transmission to a single hungry site takes years and crosses every jurisdiction in the path. The cheap renewable sources we are rightly adding are wonderful and are also, by their nature, intermittent: the sun sets, the wind drops, and a load that never pauses cannot be run on a supply that does. We are trying to feed a customer who never blinks with a system that was designed to breathe.

This is not a reason for despair. It is the clearest signal in a generation — a flare shot up over the whole energy landscape — that the world needs a new source of firm, clean, enormous power. Power that is always on, carbon-free, and can be planted next to the demand instead of wired across a continent to reach it — and needs it on a timeline measured in years, not centuries. The market is no longer theoretical. The customers are already here, already short of electrons, already willing to pay for power that is clean and always on. For the first time in the seventy-year history of fusion, the thing I have wanted to build my whole life is also the thing the world is most desperate to buy. History does that occasionally: it lines up the dream and the need, and hands the moment to whoever is ready.

What "cheap enough" really means

Now let me pour cold water on my own enthusiasm, because this book earns its wonder by refusing to cheat. It is not enough for fusion to work. Tokamaks have "worked" — produced fusion reactions — for decades. It is not even enough for fusion to break even, to give back as much energy as we spend to run it. Break-even is a laboratory result. It changes nothing about the price of your electricity, and it is worth being blunt about that from the first pages.

For fusion to matter — to reorder the world in the way the steam engine and the grid reordered it — it has to be cheap. And "cheap" has a number.

Around forty dollars per megawatt-hour — roughly four cents a kilowatt-hour — is the price at which clean, firm power stops being a premium product and becomes the obvious default. Below that line, fusion does not merely join the grid; it reorganizes it, the way cheap steel reorganized cities and cheap bandwidth reorganized commerce. Above that line, fusion is a science project with good press. The whole game, everything in this book, is getting under that line and staying there.

That is the second of this book's three forties, and it is the discipline that shapes every engineering choice that follows. We are not trying to make the most impressive fusion machine on Earth. We are trying to make the cheapest clean electron ever delivered to a grid, and to prove it with numbers anyone can check.

What lives below the line

Numbers on a page are easy to nod at and hard to feel. So let me make four cents a kilowatt-hour concrete, because the price is not the point — the price is the key, and what it unlocks is the point. Below that line, whole categories of human need that are impossible today at scale quietly become possible. Each of these gets its own treatment later in the book; here I only want you to see the shape of the door the number opens, and how much of the future is standing on the other side of it.

Water for the thirsty. The ocean is not short of water; it is short of the energy to purify it. Turning seawater into drinking water is fundamentally an energy problem — you are paying, in electricity, to pull salt away from water — and at today's power prices that bill is why desalination is a technology of wealthy, water-rich coasts rather than a birthright of the dry and poor. Drop the price of firm power under the line and the arithmetic inverts. Desalination stops being a last resort and becomes ordinary infrastructure, the way a water main is ordinary. Coastal deserts get to grow food. Cities stop rationing. The map of where humans can thrive — a map that has barely changed in five thousand years — is redrawn by a single input cost.

Heat and fuel for industry. A large share of the world's emissions do not come from lighting or driving — they come from heat, the ferocious high temperatures that make steel, cement, glass, and chemicals, produced today by burning fossil fuels because nothing else was cheap enough. Cheap, firm, clean power changes what a factory can afford to do. It makes clean industrial heat competitive with combustion. And it makes clean hydrogen — hydrogen split from water with electricity rather than stripped from natural gas — an actual commodity instead of a subsidized demonstration, because the cost of that hydrogen is, once again, mostly the cost of the electricity that makes it. Get under the line and the dirtiest, hardest-to-decarbonize third of the entire economy suddenly has a clean option it can justify on price alone.

A floor under the energy-poor. And then there are the hundreds of millions of people who still have no reliable electricity at all, and the billions more who have it but cannot count on it or afford to use much of it. For them, the price of power is not an abstraction on a bill; it is the ceiling on schooling after dark, on refrigerated medicine, on a clinic that can run a machine, on a small business that needs a motor. Every rung on that million-year ladder from the opening of this chapter is a rung these communities have not yet been allowed to climb — not for lack of will, but for lack of cheap energy. Push firm clean power under the line and you do not hand the energy-poor a charity. You hand them the same lever every prosperous society used to lift itself: abundant power at a price that no longer forces a choice between electricity and everything else.

That is what the second forty is for. Not a cheaper utility bill in a comfortable country — though it is that too — but a threshold below which water, industry, and human dignity change state, the way water itself changes state at a temperature. Cross the line and the world on the other side is genuinely, physically different. This book is a map to that line, and an argument that we finally know how to cross it.

The forty-fold return

To get there, the physics has to clear a bar that has humbled the field for seventy years — a bar that has broken more brilliant careers than any other in modern science: not break-even, but a fortyfold return. Forty units of energy out for every unit spent to run the plant. We call it Q40.

Why forty? Because a power plant is not just a reactor. It is pumps and magnets and cooling and control systems, all of which draw power, and it is a business that must sell electricity for less than it costs to make it, and still have margin left over. A machine that barely breaks even on the physics loses money on the plant. Somewhere around a fortyfold return, the arithmetic of a plant — not an experiment — finally closes. Q40 is the number where fusion stops being a marvel and becomes a product; where the fire of the stars turns into something you can put on an invoice.

I want to be honest with you from the very first chapter, because honesty is the whole method of this book: we do not have a machine producing Q40 today. No one does. What we have — what is genuinely new since I first wrote this — is a design that reaches the gain we need, solved on paper, published in the open, and deposited so that any physicist on Earth can rerun the calculation and try to break it. We stopped promising gain and started showing our work. Dazzle, then receipts: that is the deal I am making with you, page after page. The rest of this book is the work.

Let me also tell you, this early, why I care enough to spend a life on it — because a book this demanding deserves to know what its author is actually chasing underneath the arithmetic. I have believed, for as long as I can remember, that the whole point of building anything — a company, a machine, a family — is to widen the circle of who gets to be free. I have tried to live that in the most literal way I know: through a long chain of improbable kindnesses I helped bring nine children into the world across several families, only three of them my own, an arithmetic of generosity I consider the truest expression of the same idea that drives this work. Abundant, clean, cheap energy is that idea written at the scale of a planet. It is the widest circle I know how to draw. That is what Q40 is ultimately for, beneath every equation in this book: not a cheaper electron, but a far larger share of humanity handed the one input on which every other freedom is built.

The secret mathematics of a star

The title of this chapter promises to unveil the mathematics of fusion, and I mean to keep that promise, gently, before the later chapters take it apart bolt by bolt. Do not flinch at the equations. You do not need to have studied physics to follow what comes next. You need only be willing to look at a handful of equations the way you would look at a handful of well-made tools — to ask what each one is for. And there is a genuine wonder in this: the same short lines that describe the interior of every star also describe the two machines at the centre of this book. Nature wrote this grammar once, and it holds from the core of the Sun to a device you could park in a warehouse. Every fusion machine ever built or proposed is an argument conducted in these few lines. Understand what they want, and you understand the whole field.

Begin with the one equation everyone has heard of and almost no one has been shown a use for. When mass turns into energy, the exchange rate is fixed and it is staggering:

**E = Δm · c²**

Read it in plain words: the energy released (E) equals the tiny amount of mass that goes missing (Δm) multiplied by the speed of light squared (c²) — and because the speed of light is a very large number, squaring it makes the exchange rate astronomical. Here is the part that matters for fusion, and it is one of the most beautiful facts in all of physics. When two light atomic nuclei are forced to merge, the single heavier nucleus that results weighs very slightly less than the two that went in. That missing sliver of mass — the "mass defect" — has not vanished. It has been paid out as energy, at the exchange rate above. Fusion is, quite literally, the controlled conversion of a pinch of matter into a flood of energy. It is the trick the universe uses to make stars shine, reduced to an equation short enough to fit on a ring.

Now the specific reaction the near-term machines run. Take a nucleus of deuterium (heavy hydrogen, with one extra neutron) and a nucleus of tritium (hydrogen with two), drive them together hard enough, and they fuse:

D + T → ⁴He (3.5 MeV) + n (14.1 MeV)

That single event releases 17.6 million electron-volts of energy — a laughably small amount on a human scale, about a millionth of the energy in a falling raindrop, but colossal per unit of mass. Fuse a gram of this fuel and you release on the order of a million times more energy than you would get by burning a gram of coal or oil. That factor of a million is the entire prize. It is why a fusion plant's fuel bill is almost an afterthought, and why the fuel for a city's yearly power can arrive in a truck rather than a coal train that never stops. A million-to-one is not an improvement on combustion. It is a different physical regime.

Notice, too, how the 17.6 MeV splits, because that split quietly governs everything the rest of this book builds on. The helium nucleus carries 3.5 MeV and, being electrically charged, stays trapped in the magnetic bottle and helps keep the plasma hot. The neutron carries the other 14.1 MeV and, being electrically neutral, flies straight out through the magnetic field as if it were not there. One machine in this book is built to catch those neutrons and put them to work making fuel; the other is designed to make as few of them as possible. Same equation, opposite strategies — two answers to a single line of physics.

And now recall the promise at the very start of this chapter — that abundance dissolves the leverage of whoever sits on the fuel. Look at where this fuel comes from and the promise stops being rhetoric. Deuterium is simply heavy hydrogen, and it is everywhere: roughly one hydrogen atom in every six thousand in ordinary seawater is deuterium, which means the oceans hold a fuel supply measured in millions of years. Tritium does not occur in nature in useful amounts, but it can be made — bred inside the machine itself from lithium, a common element, when those escaping 14 MeV neutrons strike it. That is the deep reason fusion is a freedom technology and not merely a cleaner one. Its fuel cycle begins with two of the most common substances on Earth and manufactures the rest on site. You cannot embargo the sea. You cannot blockade a supply line to a plant that makes its own fuel from water and rock. The equation that opens this section is, read correctly, a geopolitical statement as much as a physical one.

Why two nuclei do not want to touch

If fusion is so generous, why has it taken seventy years — why is the fire of the stars still not in our cities? Because nuclei do not want to fuse. They resist with everything they have. Every nucleus carries a positive electric charge, and like charges repel. Push two of them toward each other and the repulsion rises steeply — a wall of force standing between them and the merger that would release all that energy. To climb that wall, the nuclei must be hurled at each other at tremendous speed, and speed, for a gas, is just another word for temperature. This is why fusion demands heat measured not in thousands but in hundreds of millions of degrees — far hotter than the centre of the Sun, because we cannot borrow the Sun's crushing gravity to do the squeezing for us and must make up the whole difference with sheer velocity. We are trying to out-muscle a star that outweighs us by a factor beyond imagining, using speed where it uses gravity.

Even at those temperatures the nuclei do not quite have enough energy to climb over the wall. What saves fusion — what lets the stars shine and lets us dream of copying them — is a quirk of quantum mechanics: a nucleus can occasionally pass through a barrier it lacks the energy to climb over — a kind of subatomic loophole, first quantified a century ago by a pioneer of nuclear reaction theory. Without that loophole the Sun itself would not shine, and neither would any machine we could build. Fusion lives in the narrow gap between "too cold to react at all" and "so hot the plasma destroys its own confinement." The whole art is staying inside that gap.

How often those reactions happen, how densely you can pack the fuel, and how long the magnetic bottle holds its heat before it leaks away — these are the three levers of the single most important inequality in the field, the triple product of density, temperature, and confinement time. Because the three are multiplied together, you cannot win on one alone: a blazing plasma that leaks its heat in a heartbeat clears nothing, and neither does a superbly insulated one too cold to react. You must clear the whole bar at once — and there is a further ceiling above it, set by the light a hot plasma sheds as it burns, that no magnet can spend its way past. That single bar with three levers, and the radiation ceiling that lurks just above it, are the subject of the very next section — a chapter in its own right — which takes them apart lever by lever. Here I only wanted you to see the shape of the gate: three numbers, multiplied, that must together reach one stubborn threshold.

The doubling no one advertised

Before we leave the equations, one hopeful pattern almost no one outside the laboratories knows: since the mid-1970s the triple product improved so fast that, for decades, it doubled more quickly than the transistor counts of Moore's law — fusion's own hidden exponential, running underneath the tired "thirty years away" joke. Why the machines nonetheless kept arriving late, and how high magnetic field and cheap computation finally restart that stalled climb, is a story a later chapter tells in full. Hold only the surprise for now: the field's core figure of merit was, all along, racing upward faster than the chips.

Why the equations are not yet the machine

I have shown you the tools, and now I want to be scrupulous about what they can and cannot promise, because the whole method of this book is to draw that line brightly and never smudge it. Every equation in this chapter describes the plasma — the hot, ionised fuel in the heart of the machine. None of them describes the plant wrapped around it: the magnets that must be kept colder than deep space, the pumps and cryogenics, the control electronics, the turbine that turns fusion heat back into electricity at an efficiency well below one hundred percent. Every one of those systems draws power and takes its cut before a single electron reaches the grid, and the gap between the plasma and the plant is exactly where fusion's credibility has been squandered for two generations.

That is why the fortyfold return has to be so much larger than the "break-even" the equations make sound like a finish line. Break-even is a laboratory result; it changes the physics conversation and nothing about the price of your electricity. The next chapter takes that distinction apart in full — the difference between the gain a plasma achieves and the gain a business can bank, and why only the second one matters to anyone paying a power bill. So hold the equations of this chapter the way I do: as necessary, not sufficient. They tell you what a fusion plasma must accomplish; what they cannot do is cool the magnet, close the loop from heat to electricity to profit, or build the machine. That is not physics. It is engineering, and steel, and time — and it is the subject of nearly everything that follows.

The third forty: a promise you can hold me to

Fusion has a credibility problem, and it earned it. "Thirty years away" has been the field's punchline for so long that a reasonable person could be forgiven for tuning out. I understand that reflex. I share it. It is precisely the reflex this book is built to answer — not with a louder promise, but with a more modest and more durable one.

So the third forty is not a boast. It is a horizon, deliberately drawn wide: within forty years, on a foundation that already exists, fusion power on real grids at a price that undercuts fossil fuels. My own internal plans are faster than that — considerably faster. But, as I put it in the introduction, I would rather promise the wide horizon and beat it than name a tight one and miss. You will find, throughout this book, that I have chosen the conservative number every time I had the choice. That is on purpose. In a field that has oversold itself for seventy years, restraint is not timidity — it is the only remaining form of credibility. A promise is only worth making if you can keep it.

Q40. Forty dollars a megawatt-hour. Within forty years. Three numbers, one destination — and the destination is not really about fusion at all. It is about what abundant, clean, cheap energy makes possible: water for the thirsty, power for the mind we just invented, and a century in which nations no longer have to fight over what comes out of the ground. It is the next rung on a ladder our species has been climbing for a million years, and it is the first rung that reaches up toward the stars we came from and brings their fire back down.

Energy is freedom. Everything after this chapter is the engineering of that sentence.
