A Skeptic's Questions

I wrote this book to be argued with. So before I let you go, I want to sit across the table from the sharpest version of you — the reader who has heard fusion promised before, who has watched clean-energy pitches curdle into press releases, and who came to this book with arms folded. That reader deserves better than a summary. That reader deserves the hardest questions asked plainly and answered plainly.

What follows is my attempt to ask those questions before you do, and to answer each one the way I would if you cornered me after a talk and refused to accept a slogan. I have not softened the questions to make them easier to answer. Where the honest answer is "we don't know yet," I say so. Where it is "here is the number, go check it," I give you the number. The point of this book is that the case survives scrutiny; an appendix that dodged the scrutiny would undo the point.

Isn't fusion always thirty years away?

Yes — and that punchline was earned. For most of my lifetime the field asked for patience it could not justify, promised dates it did not keep, and treated "thirty years away" as a permanent address rather than a moving target. I will not insult you by pretending that history didn't happen. It did, and it is the single largest reason a sensible person should walk into a book like this suspicious.

But there is a difference between a field that keeps missing a date and a field that has changed what kind of statement it is making. For decades, "thirty years" was a hope wearing the costume of a schedule. What I am handing you is not a hope. It is a design that is closed, published in the open, and reproducible by anyone with the training to check it — and a sequence of dated construction milestones that follow from it. The gain is solved on paper. That is a categorically different claim than "we think it might work eventually."

So I do the one thing the old promise never did: I give myself too much time on purpose. My public horizon is within forty years, while my internal planning basis reaches commercial scale around 2050 — I promise forty and plan for less, so the slack is visible to you rather than hidden from you. A field that cried "thirty years" for seventy of them has forfeited the right to a tight, flattering timeline. So I gave the timeline room to be wrong and still be kept.

You've built no hardware. Why should I believe the gain?

You shouldn't believe it because I say so. You should believe it — or refuse to — based on whether it holds up when you check it, and I have gone out of my way to make checking possible.

Here is the honest shape of the claim. We have not built a reactor. We have not lit a plasma. What we have done is close the design: solve the coupled physics of the breeder to a self-consistent operating point, at a scientific gain near 3.076, and publish the whole thing open-access so that a stranger with the right tools can reproduce it and try to break it. "Gain solved" is a statement about a design. "Gain built" is a date that has not yet arrived, and will not arrive before first operation around 2030. I keep those two sentences rigidly apart, and I get uncomfortable when anyone — including me — lets them blur.

Why trust a paper design at all, then? Because a design of this kind is not a wish; it is a set of interlocking calculations that either close or don't. Ours closes, it survived a reviewer-reply process, and every citation under it is real — zero fabricated across the entire program, which I mention because in the current climate that is no longer something you can assume. But a proof on paper is not a fire in a chamber, and the distance between them is measured in years of unglamorous engineering. I would rather walk that distance in the open than sprint it in a slide deck.

The breeder makes no electricity. So what's the point?

This is my favorite hard question, because the honest answer is exactly the thing a hype-driven pitch could never say: the first machine is net-negative on power, by roughly seventy-nine megawatts of electricity on a full engineering balance, and that is the design, not a defect we're hiding.

The point is that the breeder isn't a power plant; it's a foundry. Its product is fuel and materials — tritium, helium-3, and 14-MeV neutrons — not electrons on a wire. It breeds tritium at a target ratio near 1.8, producing on the order of four kilograms a year, and those outputs have real industrial and strategic value the day the machine runs. Anyone who tells you the first machines will pour cheap electricity onto the grid is selling the wrong decade. The first machines pour fuel.

Why build a fuel factory before a power plant? Because whoever owns the fuel owns the decade before the power plants arrive. Tritium is scarce and decaying; helium-3 is scarcer still. A machine that makes them is a business on its own terms, with no need to wait for a grid connection or a gain milestone it hasn't hit. And critically, the foundry funds the burner. The whole two-machine strategy rests on the near-term one paying for the long-term one. So the breeder making no electricity is not the awkward part of the story I hope you'll skip. It is the strategy.

Mining the Moon for fuel is science fiction, isn't it?

I understand exactly why the sentence "we will mine the Moon for fuel" sounds like a red flag, and I want to treat it as one rather than wave it away.

Here is the sober version. The burner burns deuterium and helium-3. Helium-3 barely exists on Earth — the terrestrial supply is enough to demonstrate a machine, not to fuel a fleet, and on terrestrial supply the fuel cost alone would sink the economics. The Moon, by contrast, has been soaking up helium-3 from the solar wind for billions of years; it is laid down in the lunar regolith in quantities that dwarf anything on Earth. This is not exotic physics. It is dirt with a useful isotope in it.

What I will not pretend is that this is easy or under my control. Lunar helium-3 leans on a whole adjacent industry — launch, lunar operations, extraction, return — maturing on roughly the schedule we need, around 2040. That industry is being built right now, by people who are not us, for reasons that have nothing to do with fusion. We are betting it arrives roughly on time; if it doesn't, the burner fleet is late. "Mining the Moon" is not science fiction. But it is a dependency I do not own, and I would rather you know that than discover it.

Why should I trust a number with no cost attached?

You caught the deliberate absence, so let me explain it rather than pretend it isn't there. This book states physics numbers, timeline dates, and machine names — all of which are public and published — and states no financials at all. No capital costs, no cost per megawatt-hour, no fuel prices, no margins. That is a choice, and I owe you the reasoning.

The reason is that the economics live in a confidential model that is genuinely not mine to publish. A public book that quoted capex or cost-per-electron would be either leaking numbers I'm not free to share or inventing numbers to fill the gap — and inventing numbers is exactly the sin this book exists to refuse. So I keep the ledgers out on purpose, and I would rather you distrust me for the silence than trust me for a fabrication.

But here is the thing about the number I do give you — the forty dollars a megawatt-hour that anchors the whole project. It functions in this book as a destination, not a receipt. It is the discipline that shapes every engineering choice: it is why the commercial burner is sized near a gigawatt rather than something grander and harder to finance, and it is why the fuel story runs through the Moon rather than a terrestrial supply that would blow the price apart. You are right not to take a costless number on faith. What I ask instead is that you judge the physics and the timeline — which I have shown you, in full — and hold the economics as a stated target I have not yet earned the right to prove.

What if the end-plug physics doesn't close?

Then the burner is in trouble, and I am not going to soften that. This is the one genuine physics gate standing between the design and the power fleet, and I would rather name it in a header than bury it in a footnote.

The burner is a tandem mirror — a straight magnetic bottle that traps plasma between extremely high-field plugs at each end, reaching a peak field near 26.49 tesla at the plug against a central-cell throat near 17 tesla. The whole machine lives or dies on those plugs holding enough density to confine the plasma long enough to burn. The gain is solved as a design. But the density the plugs must sustain is the hardest confinement question in the concept, and it is not yet answered by hardware. No amount of my confidence substitutes for a machine that has actually done it.

This is precisely why the program is staged the way it is. Before any commercial fleet, there is a test burner around 2032 — a roughly hundred-megawatt demonstrator whose entire job is to answer this one question with hardware instead of argument. It comes before the fleet, not after, because it would be reckless to finance gigawatt-class plants on a gate that a demonstrator hasn't closed. If the end-plug physics doesn't close as designed, the test burner is where we find out, at the smallest scale and the lowest stakes. That is what a demonstrator is for. A program that had no such gate to point at would be a program that hadn't looked hard enough.

Isn't this just another over-promising startup?

It is fair to assume so until proven otherwise, because the base rate is against me. Most ambitious energy startups over-promise; some do it cynically, most do it because enthusiasm is the fuel that raises the money, and the honest ones still tend to reach for the nearest credible date. So the burden is on me to show what's different, not on you to grant it.

Here is what I offer as evidence. First, I retired my own most exciting claim: when I first wrote this I implied a near-term net-gain milestone, and I killed it — an over-promiser adds milestones; I subtracted one. Second, I publish the miss alongside the wins — our physics-informed equilibrium solver lands at 2.5 percent error against a 1 percent target, and I print the shortfall rather than round it up. Third, the whole design is open-access and reproducible, the opposite of the closed-box pitch that asks you to trust a demo you can't inspect.

None of that proves I'll succeed; plenty of honest efforts fail. But over-promising is a specific behavior — inflating dates, hiding gates, burying misses — and I have tried to build the opposite into the structure of the book itself. Judge me on whether I keep doing it. If a number moves, I'll tell you it moved. That is the one promise I can make today that doesn't depend on a single gate closing.

Why two machines instead of one?

Because the physics wouldn't let one machine do both jobs well, and I decided that was a fact to design around rather than a fact to fight.

The job splits along the fuel. Deuterium and tritium is the easy reaction to ignite, and it is generous with neutrons — which is wonderful if what you want is neutrons and bred fuel, and terrible if what you want is clean power, because most of the energy comes out as fast neutrons that batter the walls and activate the structure. Deuterium and helium-3 is the hard reaction to ignite, and it rewards you with energy carried mostly by charged particles rather than neutrons — which is exactly what you want for clean, low-activation power that you can convert to electricity directly.

So one fuel is right for a foundry and the other is right for a power plant, and no single machine burns both well. Rather than compromise — one mediocre machine that half-breeds and half-generates — we split the labor. The breeder is a compact spherical tokamak that burns D–T and wants the neutrons, because neutrons are the product. The burner is a tandem mirror that burns D–³He and stays clean, at a neutron fraction near 5.44 percent. Each geometry follows from its fuel: the closed torus confines the easy fuel best; the open mirror lets the charged exhaust of the clean fuel stream straight into direct conversion. Two machines, two jobs, staged so the first funds the second. It is more complicated to explain than a single silver bullet — and, unlike the silver bullet, true to the physics.

The honest test of a project is whether it will answer its skeptics in public. Here are the questions, taken in good faith.

What I got wrong the first time

Several things, and I would rather list them myself than let a reader catch them.

The largest was timing and framing around gain. The 2024 version let a near-term net-gain milestone stand — a "net gain in 2027" flavor of claim — and that was wrong. 2027 is when construction begins, not when a machine produces net gain. There is no hardware net-gain milestone before first operation around 2030, and I retired the earlier claim rather than let an old enthusiasm keep collecting interest.

The second was quantum computing: I implied it would deliver a near-term advantage for this work, and it won't — not this decade. I keep that correction, and the honest near-term role for quantum, in its own question further down this appendix rather than re-arguing it twice.

The third is subtler and more about posture than fact: when I first wrote this in 2024 it was an argument, a case for a hope. In the years since, the work moved from argument to schedule — a closed design, dated construction, named gates — and the book had to be rewritten to match, because a hope and a schedule are different kinds of promise and deserve different kinds of prose. If that first version asked you to share a belief, this one asks you to audit a plan. Getting a thing wrong and printing the correction is not a weakness of this project. It is the method.

How is this different from the big public projects?

I want to answer this fairly, without naming anyone, because the big public efforts deserve respect and not a cheap contrast.

The great publicly funded machines are the cathedral projects of fusion — enormous, internationally shared, built across decades to burn deuterium and tritium and to nail down the physics of a burning plasma at full scale. Their strength is real: nearly everything the whole field knows about confining a plasma at reactor conditions, we know because those machines taught us. I am a grateful beneficiary. When they hit a milestone, it is a milestone for all of us.

Their limitation is not physics; it is scale, time, and cost. The conventional recipe asks for size, and size is slow and expensive, and it inherits the D–T neutron problem in full. Those machines were built to prove the plasma physics, not to be a template for a cheap, mass-produced power plant — and at the former they have largely succeeded.

We diverge on four things. We build a fuel foundry first and let it fund the power plant, rather than aiming a single machine at electricity. We push the neutrons upstream into the breeder, where we want them, and keep the burner clean. We convert the burner's charged-particle energy directly, instead of boiling water. And we are deliberately conservative about time. None of that makes the public machines wrong; it makes us a different bet, standing on the inheritance they paid for. If they solve the burning-plasma physics faster than expected — and they may — that is a win for the thing all of us care about.

What happens if lunar helium-3 is late?

The burner fleet is late. Full stop. I am not going to route around that, because it is the honest consequence and pretending otherwise would be exactly the move this book refuses.

Let me be precise about what is late and what isn't. Lunar helium-3 gates the scale-out of the burner fleet — the run from a handful of early burners to roughly ninety units by 2050. It does not gate the breeder, which runs on D–T and makes its own fuel starting around 2030. It does not gate the first test burner around 2032 or the earliest commercial burners, which can run on breeder-made helium-3, though in limited quantity. What lunar supply unlocks is cheap helium-3 in fleet quantities. Early burners run on the scarce, expensive stuff; the Moon is what makes the fuel abundant and the economics work at scale.

So if the Moon slips by five years, the fleet slips by roughly that, and the near-term business — the foundry — is untouched. This is one of the reasons the two-machine, materials-first structure matters so much: it puts the part I control (the breeder) early and the part I don't fully control (a whole lunar supply chain) late, so that a delay in the dependency I can't command doesn't take down the business I can. The exposure is real. It is also deliberately quarantined to the latest, most optional act of the play. I would rather build the schedule that way than one where a single external slip collapses everything.

Q_sci near 3.4 for the breeder is nowhere near Q40. Isn't that a bait-and-switch?

It would be, if I let you think the breeder was a Q40 machine. It isn't, it was never meant to be, and I say so in its own datasheet — so let me untangle the two numbers, because they answer different questions.

Q40 is the target for the burner — a fortyfold energy return, reached through low neutron losses, direct energy conversion, and those extreme plug fields. It is the number that makes a power plant a power plant. The breeder's scientific gain sits near 3.076, and that is plenty for its actual job, which is running a fuel foundry, and nowhere near enough to run a grid. That gap — between "gain that breeds fuel" and "gain that powers a civilization" — is precisely the gap the field has spent decades blurring, and I refuse to blur it.

So the honest picture is a ladder, not a single rung. The breeder's ~3.4 is real, solved, published, and sufficient for a foundry that is net-negative on electricity by design. The burner's Q40 is a design target for a different machine burning a different fuel, and it is the number that carries the eventual power and price story. Quote only the breeder's modest gain and you understate the burner; quote only Q40 and you imply the first machine hits it. Both numbers are true, about two different machines, and keeping them straight is the whole point of writing them down carefully.

If the design is "solved," why isn't it built? What does "solved on paper" even mean?

Fair suspicion — "solved" is a word that can hide a lot of daylight, so let me pin down exactly how much it claims and how much it doesn't.

"Solved on paper" means the design closes: the coupled physics — plasma, magnets, fuel cycle, heat, stress — resolves to a single self-consistent operating point that doesn't cheat any one constraint to satisfy another. It means that point is published open-access with the methods and data behind it, so an independent expert can reproduce it and attack it. It means the citations under it are real and the reviewer objections were answered. That is a genuine and non-trivial achievement, and it is the thing most fusion pitches skip straight past.

What it does not mean is that a machine exists, or that any physical object has produced this gain. A solved design is a proof that the pieces are mutually consistent and physically permitted — not a demonstration that they have been assembled and run. Between the two sits the entire engineering program: building the magnets, the vessel, the blanket, the fuel loop; commissioning them; and operating the machine to first tritium around 2030. Some things solved on paper turn out harder in metal, which is why "gain solved" and "gain built" are different sentences with different dates. The design being solved is the reason to start building. It is not a substitute for having built.

You admit disruptions aren't "retired." Isn't that a showstopper?

It's a real open problem, and I list it as one rather than quietly assume it away — but "open problem" and "showstopper" are not the same word, and the difference matters.

A disruption is a sudden, violent loss of plasma control in a tokamak: the plasma dumps its energy and current fast, and that can drive large forces on the structure and, in the worst case, beams of runaway electrons that can damage a wall. In our program's honest ledger, disruptions are not retired — the halo-current forces and runaway-electron risks are characterized but not eliminated, and I keep that on the open list where you can see it. Pretending a hard, active area of tokamak physics was fully closed would be dishonest and, frankly, unusual for anyone working in the field.

Why isn't it a showstopper? Because it is a known, bounded engineering-and-control problem, not a mystery about whether the concept can work at all. The whole field is making real progress on prediction and mitigation, and our own control layer contributes directly — a disruption-prediction ensemble reaching an area-under-curve of 0.990, a quench precursor giving forty-seven milliseconds of warning where the old method gave under three. And it belongs to the breeder, where we can design for scheduled, contingent maintenance rather than requiring perfection. It is a gate we manage, not a gate we've cleared, and I'd rather tell you it's still open than let you believe a hard problem was solved because it would be convenient if it were.

Where does the tritium come from to start? You can't breed tritium without tritium.

This is one of the sharpest questions in all of fusion, and I love that you asked it, because the naive version of the plan really does have a chicken-and-egg problem hiding in it.

A D–T machine needs tritium to run, and tritium is scarce, decays with a roughly twelve-year half-life, and can't be mined. The breeder is designed to make its own — it breeds tritium in a blanket at a ratio near 1.8, so once it's running it more than replaces what it burns. But you still have to start it, and the naive startup inventory for a machine like this is large enough that finding that much tritium up front is a genuine obstacle, not a rounding error. If the honest number were the several-kilogram figure a careless design implies, the loop would never close, because that much tritium simply isn't sitting around to be bought.

The design response is a staged startup that drives the minimum viable inventory down hard — to roughly half a kilogram, on the order of two percent of the global stockpile, rather than a quantity that doesn't exist to be acquired. That is the difference between a plan that can actually be initiated and one that assumes a resource the world can't supply. I flag it because it is exactly the kind of unglamorous, decisive detail that separates a design that has been worked through from a diagram that hasn't. The tritium bootstrap is real, it nearly sinks the naive version, and the honest design is the one that noticed and fixed it.

Deuterium-helium-3 is famously hard to ignite. Why pick the hard fuel?

Because the hard fuel is the clean fuel, and once you decide clean power is the goal, the difficulty is the price of admission, not a mistake.

D–³He is harder than D–T in every way that ignition cares about — it wants higher temperatures and it confines less forgivingly. If the only thing you optimized for was "light the fire as easily as possible," you would never choose it. But it pays you back in the currency that actually matters for a power plant: its energy comes out mostly as charged particles rather than fast neutrons, at a neutron fraction near 5.44 percent. That is the difference between a machine that slowly destroys its own walls and one that largely doesn't, and it is the difference between energy you can only capture as heat and energy you can steer directly into electricity.

So the choice is deliberate. We accept a harder ignition problem in the burner in exchange for low activation, long component life, and direct energy conversion — and we make the trade survivable by not asking the burner to also breed its own fuel. The breeder handles the hard, dirty, neutron-rich chemistry of making the fuel; the burner gets to be the clean, charged-particle machine that burns it. Picking the hard fuel only looks reckless if you imagine one machine doing everything. Split the labor, and choosing the clean-but-difficult reaction becomes the whole reason the power plant is worth building. The easy fuel would have been easier and dirtier; we wanted the clean one, and built the strategy that makes affording it possible.

Letting AI control a fusion reactor sounds reckless. Is it?

It would be reckless to let an unconstrained model improvise inside a hundred-million-degree plasma. That is not what we do, and the distinction is the whole design.

A fusion plasma fails fast — instabilities can grow in milliseconds, far quicker than a human can react. So some automated control is not optional; the only question is whether it is well-constrained. Our approach wraps the learned controllers in a control-barrier safety layer whose job is to refuse any command that would cross a hard physical limit, no matter what the model upstream wants. In testing, that clamp caught three thousand of three thousand injected limit violations with zero escapes across a hundred and fifty thousand steps. The intelligence proposes; the safety layer disposes. The machine-learning part makes the plant capable; the barrier part makes it safe, and the barrier is not something the learning is allowed to talk its way past.

And because I promised to print the misses: our physics-informed equilibrium solver lands at 2.5 percent error against a 1 percent target. It is not good enough yet, and I am not going to round it up. The surrogate models that make real-time control possible are genuinely strong — up to three orders of magnitude more accurate for the cost, the magnet twin by a factor over seven thousand — but the equilibrium solver is short of where it needs to be, and I'd rather tell you that than let the good numbers imply the whole stack is finished. AI here is a working, measured engine of control, developed with the one external partner named in this book, NVIDIA. It is powerful and it is fenced, and the fence is the point.

Notice how often the answer is not physics but engineering, money, and time — which is exactly where an honest fusion story should be.

You said quantum computing would help, and now you say it won't. Why believe you this time?

You shouldn't extend me automatic credit — but I would argue that a person who corrects their own error in print is a better bet than one who never admits to having made any.

Here is the correction, stated flatly. When I first wrote this I implied quantum computing would give this work a near-term advantage. It won't, not this decade; on the applications that matter to us, classical methods win today, and I say so plainly rather than let the old enthusiasm stand. What is genuinely near-term is quantum sensing — for diagnostics and measurement — and the computational gains we actually get come from quantum-inspired methods that run on ordinary hardware and from offline calibration, not from a quantum machine anywhere near the control loop.

Why believe the corrected version? Because it costs me something to say it, and false claims don't usually get downgraded by the person who made them — they get quietly deleted and hoped-forgotten. I'm doing the opposite: keeping the mistake visible and labeling it. The test of whether this book is trustworthy is not whether every claim turns out right; it won't, and no honest forecast does. The test is whether I flag the ones that turn out wrong. I flagged this one against my own interest. That is the behavior I'm asking you to judge me on, more than any single prediction.

Is this safe? What about radioactivity, meltdown, or weapons?

Fusion cannot melt down the way a fission reactor can — there is no critical mass and no runaway chain reaction; if you lose control, the plasma simply cools and stops. But "no meltdown" is not the same as "no hazards," and the honest safety story is about the hazards that are real.

The real one for the breeder is the tritium inventory. Tritium is radioactive and mobile, and our own bounding safety analysis is blunt about it: a worst-case bounding source term, taken bare, is not sitable — a large inventory would drive an unacceptable dose distance. That is exactly the kind of number a program can be tempted to hide. We didn't; we designed against it. The response is segmentation — breaking the inventory into small, isolated quantities so that no single release approaches the bounding case, pulling the design-relevant amount down to on the order of a kilogram. The safety case also carries several review flags that remain open, and they stay on the list rather than getting quietly closed.

On weapons: the breeder makes tritium, helium-3, and fast neutrons, and I won't pretend those materials have no strategic dimension — helium-3 in particular is nationally scarce and strategically watched, which is one reason a domestic maker of it matters. But the design is oriented toward being tritium self-sufficient, not an exporter, and the 14-MeV neutron output is a capability for materials and isotope work, not a throughput for producing weapons material. Safety here means naming the tritium hazard honestly and engineering it down, and being candid that the outputs sit near sensitive territory rather than pretending they float free of it.

Who actually needs this? Why data centers and defense sites first?

Because those are the two places where firm, clean power is worth the most and needed the soonest, and starting where the value is highest is how a hard technology earns its way into the world.

The burner comes in two housings for two customers. One is fixed defense installations — sites that need firm, resilient, on-site power that doesn't depend on a fragile grid or a fuel convoy, and that can justify a first-of-a-kind machine on the strength of that resilience. The other is data centers, which have become one of the largest and fastest-growing sources of new electricity demand on Earth, need power that is both clean and never flickers, and are increasingly straining the grids they connect to. Both need exactly what a low-neutron, firm, direct-conversion generator provides.

Starting there is not a retreat from the vision of power for everyone; it is the on-ramp to it. New energy technologies almost never arrive first as the cheapest option for everybody. They arrive first where someone needs the specific thing they offer badly enough to pay for the early units, and those units fund the learning curve that eventually makes the technology cheap for everyone. Defense and data centers are where firm clean power clears that bar today. The coastal city that wants cheap desalinated water is downstream of the fleet those first customers pay to build.

Why should a private, for-profit company own something this important?

It's a legitimate worry — concentrating something this consequential in private hands deserves scrutiny, not a shrug — so let me give you the actual reasoning rather than a platitude about markets.

The honest case for a company doing this is speed and accountability. The design closed and got published on a private timeline, with private discipline about dates and gates, in a way the largest public efforts — for structural reasons, not for lack of talent — have found hard to match. A company can be told "close the design, publish it, name the year construction begins," and be held to it. And there is a specific accountability I've built into this book: everything has a citation, a dataset, or a deposit behind it, and the whole design is open-access. Private ownership of the company does not mean private ownership of the physics. The physics is in the open, where anyone can check it and, in principle, build on it.

There is also a deliberate signal about what the abundance is for. Whatever this work earns beyond its costs is directed, by intention, to the care of elephants at The Elephant Sanctuary in Tennessee — a small, pointed reminder that the purpose of cheap, clean energy is not the enrichment of its makers but what you choose to do with the surplus. You are right to want oversight of anything this important. My answer is not "trust the company." It is "check the physics, which is public, and watch what the surplus is aimed at." I would rather be held to both than ask for faith in either.

What if a competitor gets clean power on D-T faster than you expect?

Then good — genuinely, and I mean that without the reflexive founder's bravado. If someone reaches cheap, firm, clean power before we do, by any road, the thing all of us actually care about arrives sooner, and I will be glad of it even if it isn't our machine.

Now the honest self-interested part. The scenario you're describing — the compact, high-field tokamak wave reaching clean, cheap power on deuterium and tritium faster than I expect — would make our fuel-first patience look overcautious, and I have to grant that it's possible. That wave broke the assumption that fusion must always be enormous and slow, using real physics and real magnets, and I have deep respect for it. If they win the power race on D–T, our bet on being first-to-fuel rather than first-to-power would have been the wrong bet.

But notice that even in that world, the neutron problem I keep pointing at doesn't disappear. A high-field compact tokamak is smaller and faster, and it still produces most of its energy as fast neutrons — if anything more concentrated per unit of wall. The questions of material lifetime, activation, and component replacement come back in full, and they feed straight into cost. Our wager is that once the plasma physics is broadly solved — which the whole field is converging on — the binding constraint becomes materials and fuel, not the plasma. If we're right, a fuel foundry is exactly the asset to own. If we're wrong and D–T power gets cheap fast, the world still wins. Those are acceptable terms for a bet.

The centrepost is "consumable." Doesn't that wreck uptime and economics?

It's the single biggest engineering constraint on the breeder, and it's the reason the machine is a scheduled-replacement platform rather than a set-it-and-forget-it reactor. I won't dress it up.

A spherical tokamak concentrates enormous neutron flux on the central column — the centrepost — that carries the plasma current. In our design that centrepost is consumed by the flux and must be replaced on a schedule, wearing at a rate that makes it the dominant lifetime-limiting component. That directly caps how much of the year the machine can run: the capacity factor is centrepost-contingent, and honestly so, landing in a band rather than at the reassuring ninety-plus percent people expect from a mature power plant. In plain terms: the foundry has to stop, periodically, to have its heart swapped out.

Why is that survivable? Because we designed the machine around it instead of against it. A consumable centrepost on a scheduled replacement cadence is a maintenance model, not a failure mode — closer to a component you expect to change than to an unplanned breakdown. And it's a foundry, not a grid generator, so a planned outage to swap the centrepost doesn't drop anyone's lights; it interrupts fuel production, which can be inventoried around. The cost magnitude of that replacement is real and lives in the confidential financial model, not in this public book — but I won't let the number's absence hide the physical fact. The centrepost is consumable, it's the dominant lifetime cost line, and the design's job was to make replacing it routine rather than catastrophic.

How can I check any of this myself?

This is the question I most want you to ask, because the entire posture of the book rests on the answer being "yes, you can."

The design basis is published open-access. The physics numbers I've quoted throughout — the breeder's gain near 3.076, 85.04 megawatts, 9.66 mega-amps of plasma current, an 8-tesla on-axis field, a deliberately marginal breeding lever (1.03–1.20), negative triangularity at −0.30; the burner's low neutron fraction near 5.44 percent and its plug field near 26.49 tesla — are not marketing figures I'm asking you to accept. They are the outputs of a design that was published so that people with the right training could reproduce it and try to break it. That is the difference between a claim and a result: a result comes with the means to check it.

Behind the papers sit the citations, the datasets, and the deposited artifacts. Every citation in the program is real — zero fabricated across the whole thing, a claim I can make because it was specifically audited, and one that sadly needs making in the current climate. Where a control-system result appears — the safety clamp catching three thousand of three thousand violations, the disruption ensemble at 0.990, the quench precursor at forty-seven milliseconds, and the honest 2.5-percent equilibrium miss — those are measured and deposited, not projected. You do not have to take my word for the numbers; you have to take the field's collective scrutiny for them, which is the point of publishing in the open. If a claim in this book can't be traced to something you could in principle inspect, treat it with suspicion. I tried hard not to leave any such claims in it.

What's the single most likely reason this fails?

If you made me bet against my own project, I wouldn't bet on the physics of the breeder — that's solved and buildable on gain in hand. I'd bet on one of two things downstream, and I'd want you watching both.

The first is the burner's end-plug density gate. It is the one genuine physics problem left to close, and if the test burner around 2032 finds that the plugs can't hold what the design needs, the clean-power half of the whole vision is in serious trouble. That's why the demonstrator exists and comes first. The second is the lunar helium-3 supply chain — a dependency I don't control, riding on an adjacent industry maturing roughly on our schedule around 2040. If that industry is late, the burner fleet is late; if it never really materializes at the cost required, the fleet economics don't close, and the breeder foundry becomes the durable business while the grand power story stalls.

Notice that neither of my top failure modes is "the design doesn't work." That's deliberate — the design working is the part we've actually closed. The risks that keep me honest are a hardware physics gate we haven't demonstrated yet and an external supply chain we don't command. I could be wrong about which one bites; something I'm confident about could surprise me, because that is what building hard things is like. But if this project fails, my honest guess is that it fails at the plug or on the Moon — and I'd rather have told you exactly where to look than have you find out on your own.

Why "within forty years" if you actually plan for 2050?

Because the gap between the promise and the plan is a feature, and I want it sitting in the open where you can use it against me.

My internal planning basis reaches commercial scale around 2050 — inside three decades from first construction in 2027. My public promise is within forty years. That gap is not confusion and it is not hedging for its own sake; it is deliberate slack, and it exists because of the exact history that opened this appendix. A field that cried "thirty years away" for seventy of them has no business offering a tight, flattering timeline, because a tight timeline is precisely the thing that field kept breaking. So I do the opposite of the traditional move: I promise more time than I think I need, and I plan for less.

The logic is simple and, I hope, disarming. If I beat the clock — and I intend to — you have lost nothing by my caution; you get the thing early. If the hard parts prove harder than I hope, the slack is already visible in the schedule, so a slip lands inside the promise instead of breaking it. Under-promising here isn't modesty or a marketing trick. It's a design constraint on my own behavior, meant to make it structurally difficult for me to over-promise the way the field taught you to expect. The three forties — Q40 for the physics, forty dollars a megawatt-hour for the price, within forty years for the honesty — are built so the last of them protects you from the first two. That is the whole point of writing the horizon down conservatively and then daring you to hold me to it.

If a question here has no good answer, that is a finding too. We would rather you found it in these pages than after you trusted us.

Two machines means two things that can break. Isn't that twice the risk?

I hear the real objection under the question: two machines means two devices that can fail, two physics programs, two schedules to slip — so haven't I simply doubled my own exposure? It's a fair piece of accounting, and I want to answer it as accounting rather than rhetoric.

Yes, there are two machines. No, the risk does not simply double, because they are staged in series and firewalled from each other, not wired into a single fragile chain. The breeder — Hyperion — is a D–T spherical tokamak whose physics is solved, whose gain near 3.076 is in hand on paper, and whose job is to make fuel and money starting around 2030. The burner is a later, harder machine with one genuine open gate. Had I fused both jobs into one device, every risk would land on a single object at a single moment: I'd be betting the whole enterprise on the hardest fuel and the hardest confinement problem simultaneously, with nothing earning while I did it.

Splitting them lets the near-term, lower-risk machine fund and de-risk the far-term, higher-risk one. The failure surfaces are separated in time, so a stumble on the burner doesn't take down the foundry. Two machines is more to build. It is also less to lose at once.

How are you different from the well-funded private companies chasing compact tokamaks?

Let me take the question seriously, because on the surface we look like one more venture-backed fusion company with a bold deck, and you're right to ask what actually separates us.

The compact-tokamak wave — private, ambitious, well-financed — is mostly aiming a single high-field machine straight at cheap electricity on deuterium and tritium. I admire the nerve of it and I've learned from it. But it's a fundamentally different bet than ours, in three ways. First, we don't aim one machine at electricity; we build a fuel foundry first and let it fund the power plant, so we have a near-term business — materials, not electrons — that doesn't hang on winning the power race. Second, we push the neutrons upstream, into the breeder where we want them, and keep the burner clean at a neutron fraction near 5.44 percent, instead of accepting a wall-battering neutron flux in the very machine meant to sell power. Third, the design is closed and published open-access, with a granted patent and real citations, rather than a closed demo you're asked to trust.

None of that makes them wrong. It makes us a different animal: materials-first, two-machine, and wagering that fuel, not plasma, is the binding constraint the field is walking toward.

Isn't the end-plug density requirement just hand-waving?

No — but I understand why it can smell that way, so let me show you the shape of the number instead of asking you to trust the word "solved."

The burner is a tandem mirror: a straight bottle that plugs each end with an intense magnetic field — a peak near 26.49 tesla at the plug against a central-cell throat near 17 tesla — and the plugs must hold enough density to keep the plasma confined long enough to burn. That density is the single hardest confinement demand in the concept. Hand-waving would be naming a comfortable figure and moving on. What we did instead is specify it as a concrete operating requirement inside a design that closes — the plug fields, the density, the confinement, and the burn all resolving to one self-consistent point rather than each being wished into place.

The honest part is that a requirement specified on paper is not the same as hardware having met it. That is exactly why the roughly 100-megawatt test burner around 2032 comes before any fleet: its entire job is to put this one number under a real machine. The requirement is precise, quantified, and testable. That is the opposite of hand-waving — it's a gate with a date.

Why should a data center bet on unproven fusion over gas with carbon capture?

It shouldn't — not today, and I'd distrust anyone who told a data-center operator to rip out a working plan for a machine that hasn't run. The honest comparison is about the decade these choices actually play out in.

Gas with carbon capture is real, buildable now, and a legitimate bridge. Its costs, though, are the ones that never leave: you still buy fuel forever, you still emit whatever the capture rate misses, you still carry the exposure of a commodity price and a carbon liability that only tightens with time. A firm, low-neutron fusion generator — the MetroVolt housing built for exactly this customer — offers something gas-plus-capture structurally can't: firm power with no carbon stream to manage and a fuel path aimed at forty dollars a megawatt-hour rather than a fuel bill that floats with the market.

So the bet isn't "unproven fusion instead of gas tomorrow." It's whether a data center that already needs firm clean power for decades wants to help fund the early units that make the clean, firm option cheap — the way the first buyers of any hard technology do. Early adopters don't buy fusion because it's proven. They buy it because they need the specific thing it will become, and can afford to be early.

What could a competitor copy from the published design — and why publish it at all?

This is the question that makes investors wince, so let me answer it head-on: yes, publishing the design open-access means a competent rival can read the physics, and no, I don't think that was a mistake.

What's public is the design basis — the operating point, the methods, the numbers I've quoted throughout — deposited so a stranger can reproduce it and try to break it. What that buys us is worth more than the secrecy it costs. A closed design is one you're asked to believe; a published one is a result the field can check, and in a domain where the base rate is over-promising, verifiability is the scarcest asset a company can own. The physics being open is precisely why you should trust the physics.

Copying it is harder than reading it. The granted patent (US 12,009,112) protects the core invention; the years of unglamorous engineering between a solved design and a running machine are their own moat; and the fuel-first strategy, the lunar supply path, and the control stack built with NVIDIA are execution, not a diagram anyone can lift. I'd rather win by being the ones who actually build it, in the open, than by guarding a sketch. Secrecy protects claims that can't survive daylight. Ours can.

What would actually make you walk away from this approach?

I want to answer this because a project with no stated kill-criteria isn't a plan, it's a faith, and I promised you the former. So here are the things that would make me stop, named in advance.

If the roughly 100-megawatt test burner around 2032 shows the end-plug density can't be reached or held — not "is hard," but genuinely cannot be sustained in hardware after honest effort — then the clean-power half of this vision is dead as designed, and I would not keep raising money against a gate a demonstrator had closed against me. If the breeder reached first tritium and the fuel cycle simply didn't close — if the breeding ratio came in far under the design near 1.8 and stayed there — the foundry thesis itself would be in serious question. And if the equilibrium and control stack never became trustworthy enough to run the plant safely, I would not hand a hundred-million-degree plasma to a controller I didn't believe.

Notice these are specific, dated, and measurable, not vibes. That's the point of writing them down: it makes it harder for me to move the goalposts later. I intend to clear every one of them. But if I can't, I'd rather retire the approach honestly than let it become the thing this book was written against.

If the Moon never delivers cheap helium-3 — not just late — is there a Plan B?

Elsewhere in this appendix I answered what happens if lunar helium-3 arrives late. You're asking something harder: what if it never comes at all, or never gets cheap enough to matter? I won't pretend that isn't a real branch of the future.

Here is what survives even in that world. The breeder — Hyperion — never needed the Moon; it runs on deuterium and tritium and breeds its own fuel starting around 2030, so the near-term foundry business is untouched. The earliest test burner and first commercial burners can run on breeder-made helium-3, in scarce and expensive quantity. What the Moon uniquely unlocks is cheap helium-3 in fleet quantities — the thing that carries the forty-dollar-a-megawatt-hour power story from a handful of units to a real fleet. Without it, the burner doesn't vanish; it stays a premium machine for customers who value firm, clean, low-neutron power enough to pay for scarce fuel — defense sites and data centers, the very first customers by design.

So the Plan B is not a secret second Moon. It's that the enterprise was deliberately built so its floor is the foundry, which needs no Moon at all, and only its most expansive ceiling — cheap power for everyone — leans on lunar supply. If the Moon fails, we lose the ceiling, not the building.

Direct energy conversion sounds too good to be true. Has anyone actually done it?

It does sound like a magic trick — skip the boiler, the steam, the turbine, and turn fusion products straight into electricity — so you're right to poke it.

The physics isn't magic; it's old. Charged particles carry electric charge, and a charged particle flying away from you is a current you can decelerate against a voltage and harvest directly, without ever making heat. The reason nobody runs power plants this way is that most fusion and fission dump their energy into neutral carriers — neutrons, or heat — which have no charge to grab. This is the whole reason we chose the hard, clean fuel: D–³He releases its energy mostly as charged particles, at a neutron fraction near 5.44 percent, which is exactly the stream direct conversion can catch. The tandem-mirror geometry helps too, because its open end lets the charged exhaust stream straight out into a conversion train instead of into a wall.

The honest caveat: direct conversion has been demonstrated in pieces and at small scale, not yet as a mature, high-efficiency stage on a burning D–³He machine at power. It's an engineering program, not a finished product, and its efficiencies are a design target I still have to earn. But it isn't exotic physics. It's a known idea whose fuel finally fits.

The burner is about 440 meters long. Isn't that too big to mass-produce?

You caught the tension, and I'd be annoyed if you hadn't: I spend this book criticizing the great machines for being enormous, and then my burner runs on the order of 440 meters end to end. That deserves an answer, not a dodge.

The length is a consequence of the geometry, not a vanity. A tandem mirror is a straight machine — a long central cell capped by plugs at each end — and openness is the feature, because it's what lets the charged exhaust stream out into direct conversion instead of into a wall. You trade compactness for a clean, linear power path. So yes, it's long. But long is not the same as massive and bespoke the way a cathedral-scale tokamak is. A linear machine is modular almost by definition: repeated central-cell sections between two engineered ends. That is a far friendlier thing to build over and over than a single giant torus.

I won't pretend a 440-meter machine is small, or that manufacturing it at fleet scale is trivial — it isn't, and the learning curve is real. But the objection that "big equals slow and unrepeatable" was about monolithic size, not length. A long, sectioned, modular machine is exactly the kind of big that mass production can actually get its arms around.

The point of a book that shows its work is that you never have to take the author's word for the part that matters.

Negative triangularity at −0.30 — why the exotic plasma shape, and is it proven?

Fair to flag it: negative triangularity is an unusual shaping choice, and "unusual" in fusion is a reasonable place to point a skeptical finger.

Triangularity describes the cross-sectional shape of the plasma — whether its D-profile points toward or away from the machine's center. Most designs run positive triangularity. We hold the breeder at a negative value near −0.30 because that shaping helps manage the heat and stability at the plasma edge — it can suppress some of the violent edge instabilities that otherwise hammer a reactor wall — which matters enormously in a machine whose entire job is to survive an intense neutron and heat environment long enough to breed fuel. It's a deliberate choice in service of component life, not novelty for its own sake.

Is it proven? Negative triangularity is an active, respected line of experimental fusion research, not a private invention of ours — real machines have run it and shown real edge benefits. What is not yet proven is our specific integrated operating point at reactor conditions, because no reactor-scale version of this design has been built. That's the honest line I keep drawing through the whole book: the shaping physics is credible and studied; the machine that combines it with everything else is still ahead of us, with first tritium around 2030.

Are the high-field REBCO magnets actually mature — or is that another unproven bet?

It's the right component to interrogate, because the magnets are where a lot of this design's ambition is spent — a high-field REBCO centrepost in the breeder (8 tesla on-axis), and near 26.49 tesla at the burner's plugs. Those are not gentle numbers.

The enabling technology is REBCO — a high-temperature superconducting tape that carries enormous current at high field, and it's the same materials advance that let the whole modern wave of compact, high-field fusion machines exist at all. This is genuinely one of the more mature bets in the design, not one of the shakier ones: high-field REBCO magnets have moved from laboratory curiosity to demonstrated large-bore, record-field magnets in recent years, which is precisely why designs like ours are creditable now and weren't a decade ago. On-axis the breeder field is a calm 8 tesla; the peak at the conductor is substantially higher.

What remains real work is engineering these magnets for a reactor's lifetime under neutron flux, mechanical stress, and thermal cycling — durability and manufacturability at scale, not whether the field can be reached at all. So I'd put the magnets on the "hard engineering, not open physics" side of the ledger. The bet isn't whether high-field superconductors work. It's building them to last in the least forgiving environment we know how to make.

Ninety machines by 2050 — can you actually manufacture at that scale?

I'll grant the skepticism before I answer it: getting from a first-of-a-kind machine to something like ninety units in a couple of decades is a manufacturing claim as much as a physics one, and the graveyard of energy technology is full of things that worked once and never scaled.

Two design choices are aimed squarely at this. The first is that the burner is a linear, sectioned machine rather than a single monolithic vessel — a long central cell of repeated modules between two engineered ends — the kind of architecture that rewards a production line instead of demanding a decade-long bespoke build each time. The second is the staging itself: a roughly 100-megawatt test burner around 2032, then a commercial roughly 1-gigawatt fleet from around 2036, then scale-out toward 2050. That ramp exists precisely so the manufacturing learning happens on a few machines before it has to happen on many.

I won't wave away the difficulty. Building complex machines at fleet cadence is its own discipline, and the fuel supply has to scale in step, which is why lunar helium-3 around 2040 sits where it does. But ninety machines is a target the schedule was shaped around from the start, not a number I appended to sound ambitious. Whether we hit it is a thing you get to watch, unit by dated unit.
